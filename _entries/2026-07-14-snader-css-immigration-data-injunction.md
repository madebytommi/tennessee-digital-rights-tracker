## Research and legal limitations

This entry is public-record research, not legal advice. The legal dispute concerns the interaction among Tennessee public-benefit law, federal immigration and benefits law, healthcare-program requirements, administrative procedure, and privacy protections. The court has not issued a final ruling on the merits.

The project and its contributors:

- do not have access to individual Children’s Special Services records, immigration files, clinical records, internal agency databases, or the restricted state reporting form;
- cannot determine from public sources whether a particular child or household is lawfully present, eligible for a benefit, or affected by Public Chapter 1106;
- cannot verify nonpublic system configurations, report fields, transmission logs, access logs, retention schedules, or downstream disclosures;
- do not claim that complete medical charts were intended for transfer; and
- do not treat the temporary injunction as a permanent invalidation of Public Chapter 1106.

No child, parent, household, patient, or provider should be identified through this project. Unknowns are stated explicitly rather than filled with assumptions.

## What happened?

Governor Bill Lee signed Senate Bill 1915, enacted as Public Chapter 1106, on May 22, 2026. Administrative work needed to implement the law took effect immediately, while the remaining provisions took effect July 1.

The act amended Tennessee’s Eligibility Verification for Entitlements Act. Among other changes, it expanded the law to local governmental entities and directed covered state agencies, local governments, and local health departments to report certain public-benefit recipients and identifying information to the Centralized Immigration Enforcement Division within the Tennessee Department of Safety and Homeland Security, except where federal law prohibits reporting.

The Tennessee Department of Health interpreted the law as applying to Children’s Special Services, or CSS. CSS is a statewide program for children and young adults with chronic illnesses or disabilities. It provides care coordination and may reimburse medical services, prescriptions, equipment, supplies, therapies, and related support when eligibility requirements are met.

On June 1, the department sent notices to affected families and healthcare providers. The family notice said that, based on the department’s records and the child’s current immigration status, continued CSS payment for healthcare after June 30 would result in the child’s information being shared with the Department of Safety. The provider notice said the department would submit identifying information for patients listed on an attached spreadsheet if CSS continued paying for services after June 30.

Three physicians filed *Snader v. Tennessee Department of Health* in Davidson County Chancery Court on June 24. They challenged the department’s interpretation and implementation, alleging that it conflicted with state and federal law and threatened serious harm by causing families to leave medically necessary care.

The court entered a temporary restraining order on June 24. After extensions and agreed interim orders, Chancellor Patricia Head Moskal entered an Agreed Temporary Injunction on July 14 in case number 26-0825-I.

The injunction currently bars the Department of Health from reporting identifying information about affected CSS participants to the Centralized Immigration Enforcement Division under Public Chapter 1106. It also requires the department to restore and maintain covered enrollment, recertify affected participants without considering lawful presence, and notify families and providers about the order.

The injunction remains in place until further order of the court. The order stays the court proceedings, including discovery, while a petition for a declaratory order filed with the Department of Health remains pending. The state did not admit liability or wrongdoing, and the plaintiffs did not waive their claims.

## What the primary source says

### Public Chapter 1106 and the adopted replacement amendment

The adopted House replacement amendment added Tennessee Code § 4-58-112. It directs covered state governmental entities, local governmental entities, and local health departments to report individuals and “all identifying information” about individuals who:

1. are determined not to be lawfully present under the federal Immigration and Nationality Act; and
2. receive a federal, state, or local public benefit from the reporting entity.

The reporting duty begins with an exception for disclosures prohibited by federal law. The provision sends reports to the Centralized Immigration Enforcement Division unless a separate federal food-assistance reporting rule applies.

Other sections of the act address verification for applicants who are 18 or older, preservation of verification documentation, termination of recurring benefits after final verification, and state enforcement against local entities. How the age-18 language interacts with the broader wording of § 4-58-112 is one of the disputed legal questions.

The adopted House replacement amendment, which the Senate formally concurred in before the bill became Public Chapter 1106, retains a criminal provision. Section 4-58-112(b)(1) makes it an offense for an employee or official to intentionally fail to make a required report about a person the employee or official knows is not lawfully present, and subsection (b)(2) classifies a violation as a Class A misdemeanor. The General Assembly’s narrative bill summary says the House amendment removed that offense, but that summary conflicts with the adopted amendment, the recorded concurrence, and the 2026 Index of Acts published by the University of Tennessee County Technical Assistance Service. This entry follows the enacted amendment rather than the narrative summary. The unusual cross-reference wording in subsection (b)(2) may require legal interpretation, but the presence of the misdemeanor language is not treated as uncertain.

The act separately exempts the Tennessee Department of Children’s Services from providing information that would directly or indirectly identify a child or family receiving that department’s services. That exemption names the Department of Children’s Services, not the Department of Health or the CSS program.

### Tennessee Department of Health notices

The June 1 family notice stated that Public Chapter 1106 required the Department of Health to report patients receiving healthcare who were not legally present and said the reports would go to the immigration division in the Department of Safety.

The notice did not list the exact fields that would be transmitted. It referred generally to sharing the child’s “information.”

The provider notice was more specific about the legal category of information. It stated that the department would submit “identifying information” for patients identified on an attached spreadsheet if CSS continued paying for their services after June 30.

The public copy of the provider notice does not establish the contents of the attached spreadsheet, and this project has not sought or reviewed any patient list.

Neither public notice says that complete clinical notes, diagnoses, treatment plans, claims histories, or medical charts would be transmitted. The notices do show that the planned report would be linked to continued participation in a healthcare-assistance program.

### The agreed temporary injunction

The July 14 order enjoins the Department of Health and its commissioner from reporting identifying information about CSS patients whom the department determined were not lawfully present.

It further requires the department to:

- disregard withdrawal requests for affected children who were enrolled as of June 1;
- restore their CSS status to what it was on June 1;
- prevent removal based on the department’s lawful-presence determination;
- recertify recipients of the June 1 letter under existing CSS criteria without considering lawful presence;
- notify affected patients and providers in the patient’s primary language; and
- post updated information and a copy or link to the injunction on the CSS webpage.

The order contains no final findings of fact or conclusions of law. It records an agreement between the parties, stays discovery while an administrative petition is pending, and expressly states that the defendants do not admit wrongdoing or liability.

### The state reporting process

The Centralized Immigration Enforcement Division’s official page says its Public Chapter 1106 reporting form is restricted and not publicly downloadable.

An authorized entity must identify the agency, requesting official, governmental-entity type, program, and public benefit involved. After verification, the division provides a secure fillable form, a unique agency password, and submission instructions. Completed reports must be returned through the secure method supplied by the division, and redistribution of the form or password is prohibited.

The public page does not identify every report field or explain:

- how long reports are retained;
- who can view, download, correct, or delete them;
- whether reports are forwarded to federal immigration authorities;
- how duplicate or incorrect identities are resolved;
- what audit reviews occur; or
- what redress process is available to a person whose information is inaccurate.

A secure submission method reduces some transmission risks but does not answer whether the disclosure is lawful, necessary, accurate, minimized, retained appropriately, or shared further.

## What officials say

The Department of Health’s June 1 notices reflect the agency’s initial position that Public Chapter 1106 applied to CSS participants identified in its records. The notices warned that continued program payment after June 30 would trigger reporting to the Department of Safety.

The agreed injunction reflects a later temporary arrangement under which the department will not make those reports and will maintain affected enrollment while the dispute proceeds. The order states that the department and commissioner do not concede liability, wrongdoing, or any legal claim.

The Centralized Immigration Enforcement Division describes the reporting process as restricted and secure. It says the division verifies requesting agencies and supplies a password-protected form and secure return instructions.

Tennessee Lookout reported that bill sponsor Senator Ed Jackson expressed reservations about applying the law to children in CSS and said he was considering possible adjustments. That reported statement is not itself a legislative amendment or binding interpretation.

As of the last review date, this project did not locate a public, detailed merits explanation from the Department of Health or Attorney General addressing the physicians’ statutory, federal-law, funding, or administrative-procedure arguments.

## What advocates and critics say

The physician plaintiffs and the Tennessee Justice Center argue that the department misread Public Chapter 1106, particularly because other provisions expressly address applicants who are 18 or older.

They also argue that federal requirements connected to CSS and the Maternal and Child Health Services Block Grant restrict the state’s approach, and that the department adopted the policy without required administrative procedures.

The plaintiffs say the notices caused or threatened severe harm by forcing families to choose between continued care and possible immigration-enforcement exposure. They also question whether the state accurately classified all affected children’s immigration status.

Those are pending claims, not final court findings. The agreed injunction preserves the plaintiffs’ arguments but does not resolve them.

Supporters of Public Chapter 1106 describe it more broadly as an eligibility-verification and immigration-enforcement measure for public benefits. The law’s application to CSS—and especially to children—has generated a narrower dispute that the General Assembly did not resolve in the statutory text with program-specific instructions.

## Who may be affected?

The challenged policy and injunction may affect:

- children and young adults receiving CSS services;
- children with serious chronic illnesses, physical disabilities, developmental needs, or complex medical conditions;
- families in immigrant or mixed-status households;
- children whose lawful-presence status may be disputed, incomplete, or incorrectly recorded;
- physicians and other healthcare providers responsible for continuity of care;
- local health departments and staff administering CSS;
- Department of Health staff handling eligibility, recertification, payments, and reporting;
- Department of Safety personnel responsible for receiving public-benefit reports;
- organizations providing disability, pediatric-health, immigrant-rights, legal, and privacy assistance; and
- the broader group of people applying for public benefits through state agencies, local governments, or local health departments under Public Chapter 1106.

The court order directly addresses the identified CSS participants and the Department of Health’s implementation. It does not suspend every part of Public Chapter 1106 or every reporting process involving another program or agency.

## Privacy and civil-liberties significance

This dispute does not require proof that complete medical charts were going to immigration enforcement to raise serious health-data concerns.

A record can reveal sensitive information through context. Identifying a person as a participant in a program available specifically for children and young adults with chronic illnesses or disabilities may reveal or strongly imply health and disability information even when no diagnosis or clinical note is attached.

The planned process also connects systems with different purposes:

- a healthcare-assistance and care-coordination program;
- benefit-eligibility and immigration-status verification;
- a restricted state reporting form; and
- an immigration-enforcement division that coordinates with federal authorities.

When information moves between such systems, important questions include purpose limitation, data minimization, accuracy, role-based access, retention, audit logging, correction, redress, and downstream use.

The notices also show a potential chilling effect. A family may avoid or leave a health program because participation itself creates a perceived enforcement risk. That effect can reach lawful-status and mixed-status households as well as the people the agency intended to report.

The most defensible current conclusion is:

> Tennessee attempted to connect identifying information from a specialized children’s healthcare-assistance program to a state immigration-enforcement reporting process. A court has temporarily blocked that application and protected continued enrollment, but the litigation has not resolved the law’s meaning, the federal limits, the intended data fields, or the state’s broader reporting practices.

The injunction provides immediate protection for the affected CSS participants. It does not answer what happened inside agency systems before the order, how the reporting infrastructure operates across other programs, or what rules will govern the data if the injunction changes.

## Lawful actions and resources

- Read the July 14 Agreed Temporary Injunction and the June 1 family and provider notices rather than relying only on summaries.
- Check the Tennessee Justice Center litigation page and the Department of Health’s CSS webpage for later orders or administrative decisions.
- Families affected by CSS should rely on current official notices and obtain advice from a qualified attorney or established legal-services organization for individual circumstances.
- Request only non-patient-level public records, such as blank forms, data dictionaries, aggregate counts, workflow diagrams, retention policies, audit procedures, legal memoranda, and injunction-compliance instructions.
- Do not request or publish names, addresses, immigration identifiers, diagnoses, provider-patient relationships, spreadsheets of affected patients, or individual benefit records.
- Ask the Department of Health whether any reports were prepared or transmitted before June 24 and request aggregate remediation records rather than individual data.
- Ask the Centralized Immigration Enforcement Division for its report-field list, retention schedule, access roles, audit policy, correction process, and downstream-sharing rules, allowing lawful redaction of credentials and security-sensitive details.
- Review future General Assembly action and court rulings for clarification of the age threshold, program coverage, federal-law exception, misdemeanor provision, and application to other public-benefit programs.
- Consult a qualified Tennessee attorney for legal advice concerning Public Chapter 1106, immigration status, public-benefit eligibility, or disclosure of a particular person’s information.
