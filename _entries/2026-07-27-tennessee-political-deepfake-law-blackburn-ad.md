## What happened?

During its 2026 session, the Tennessee General Assembly enacted HB 1513 / SB 1624 as Public Chapter 625, titled the **Transparency for Deepfakes in Political Advertising Act**. The official bill history says Governor Bill Lee signed the measure on March 26, 2026, the Secretary of State assigned the public chapter on March 30, and the law took effect on July 1, 2026.

The measure amended Tennessee’s political-advertising disclaimer statute by adding requirements for certain audio and video communications that qualify as deepfakes and impersonate or depict a candidate engaging in speech or action that did not occur.

In late July 2026, a television advertisement titled **“Queen Pharma Mama”** criticized U.S. Senator and Tennessee gubernatorial candidate Marsha Blackburn over pharmaceutical legislation and political contributions. A surviving AdImpact record identified American Exceptionalism Institute as the advertiser and categorized the spot as an election advertisement. Axios reported that the advertisement used an obviously AI-generated depiction of Blackburn while a jingle made disputed allegations about pharmaceutical interests and legislation.

On July 27, an attorney representing Blackburn sent a cease-and-desist and evidence-preservation letter to American Exceptionalism Institute Chairman Wayne Ducote and consultant Joel Riter. Tennessee Lookout reported that the letter denied the advertisement’s allegations, demanded that distribution stop, sought confirmation within 48 hours, requested preservation of evidence, and asked whether artificial intelligence, synthetic media, deepfake technology, digital manipulation, or similar tools had been used.

Axios reported, based on information from Blackburn’s campaign, that Comcast and Viamedia removed the advertisement. This review did not locate an independent statement from either company explaining whether a removal occurred, which version was affected, or whether the decision resulted from legal concerns, advertiser action, substantiation rules, scheduling, or another reason.

The AdImpact viewer page previously linked in reporting now identifies the advertisement as deleted and no longer available for viewing. A public Reddit discussion contains a comment from a user who said they recorded the advertisement from WKRN during *Wheel of Fortune* and attached a video. The project could locate that recovery lead but could not retrieve or authenticate the attached media during this review.

A demand letter is not a lawsuit, criminal charge, agency complaint, or official finding. A fresh search through July 31 did not locate a public advertiser response, civil complaint, injunction request, criminal charge, agency decision, or court order resolving whether the law applied or was violated.

## What the primary source says

### Covered communications and required disclosures

Public Chapter 625 added subdivision (a)(6) to Tennessee Code § 2-19-120.

For a covered **audio communication**, the disclosure rule applies when the communication contains a deepfake, as defined in Tennessee Code § 39-17-1902, and impersonates a candidate engaging in speech the candidate did not make. The communication must include a clear notice at both its beginning and end stating that it contains fake content impersonating speech that did not occur.

For a covered **video communication**, the rule applies when the communication contains a statutory deepfake and depicts a candidate engaging in action or speech in which the candidate did not engage. The law requires a prescribed notice in large letters for the duration of the video. The wording differs depending on whether the video depicts nonexistent action only or both nonexistent action and speech.

The enacted text therefore does not create a general prohibition on AI-assisted political advertising. It imposes disclosure requirements when the communication falls within the statute and contains the specified form of synthetic depiction.

### Incorporated definition of “deepfake”

The political-advertising law incorporates the definition in Tennessee Code § 39-17-1902. Tennessee’s official legislative summary describes a deepfake as a video, image, or audio file generated or manipulated by artificial intelligence to **realistically portray something that did not actually occur**.

That definition creates unresolved factual questions in this dispute:

1. Was the Blackburn image generated or manipulated through artificial intelligence, rather than conventional illustration, compositing, or editing?
2. Did the image realistically portray a nonexistent event?
3. Was Blackburn depicted personally speaking or acting, or was a synthetic still image merely displayed while a separate narrator or jingle made allegations?
4. Was any synthetic voice intended to impersonate Blackburn?

Axios’s description of an “obviously AI-generated” image supports investigating AI use. It does not, by itself, establish that the depiction satisfied the statutory requirement of realistic portrayal.

### Relationship to the existing political-advertising statute

Subdivision (a)(6) was inserted into a statute that already requires payor and authorization disclaimers for certain communications financed to expressly advocate the election or defeat of a clearly identified candidate or solicit contributions. The new subdivision begins by referring to “such communication.”

The surviving transcript attributed to the advertisement reportedly ends by urging viewers to contact Blackburn rather than using an obvious vote-for, vote-against, elect, or defeat instruction. Whether the full advertisement qualified as the type of communication covered by subsection (a) is an unresolved textual and factual question. The transcript may be incomplete or inaccurate, and the complete video has not been independently reviewed by this project.

### Candidate and timing provision

When the impersonated or depicted candidate is not an incumbent office holder, the new disclosure provisions apply only if the communication is distributed within 120 days of the initiation of voting in an election in which the candidate appears on the ballot.

Blackburn was a sitting U.S. senator and a candidate in Tennessee’s August 6 gubernatorial primary when the advertisement was distributed. The statute does not expressly resolve whether “incumbent office holder” means an incumbent in any public office or an incumbent in the particular office being sought. Even if Blackburn were treated as a non-incumbent candidate for governor, the late-July distribution occurred within 120 days of voting.

### Liability and remedies

A violation of Tennessee Code § 2-19-120 is classified as a Class C misdemeanor.

Public Chapter 625 also gives an impersonated or depicted candidate access to specified damages and equitable relief under Tennessee Code § 39-17-1904, with an exception concerning that section’s pseudonym provision. How those remedies operate in a political-advertising dispute has not yet been interpreted in a located Tennessee court decision.

The adopted legislation limits liability for intermediaries. Internet hosts and service providers are not liable solely for providing access or transmission when they did not create the deceptive deepfake. Radio and television broadcasters, cable and satellite operators, streaming services, and other programming services paid to distribute the material are likewise protected to the extent they were not responsible for creating it.

## What officials say

The Tennessee General Assembly’s official summary describes the act as requiring disclaimers for political advertisements containing deepfake elements that impersonate or depict a candidate engaging in speech or conduct that did not occur. The official record identifies July 1, 2026, as the effective date.

Blackburn’s attorney contends that the advertisement made false and defamatory allegations involving bribery and corruption. Tennessee Lookout reported that the letter also sought disclosure of whether synthetic or AI-generated material was used and argued that undisclosed manipulation could compound the alleged harm.

Those assertions remain allegations made on Blackburn’s behalf. This entry does not determine whether the advertisement’s political claims were defamatory, whether the advertiser acted with actual malice, or whether the deepfake-disclosure statute applied.

Tennessee Lookout reported that it could not reach American Exceptionalism Institute’s officers for comment. A fresh search did not locate a later public response from the organization, Ducote, Riter, a production company, or identified legal counsel.

Axios reported that Comcast and Viamedia removed the advertisement according to Blackburn’s campaign. No direct company statement explaining the reported action was located.

## What advocates and critics say

This review did not locate a Tennessee civil-liberties organization’s published analysis applying Public Chapter 625 to this specific advertisement.

Supporters of synthetic-media disclosures generally argue that clear labeling can help voters distinguish authentic recordings from fabricated depictions without banning political criticism or prohibiting the use of AI tools.

Civil-liberties and free-speech concerns can arise when a law’s definitions are unclear or when compelled labels reach satire, parody, editorial illustration, commentary, or other expression that reasonable viewers would not mistake for authentic footage. This entry does not resolve those constitutional questions. A court’s analysis would depend on the law’s text, the complete advertisement, the context in which it appeared, and the relief sought.

The missing advertisement also illustrates a preservation problem. Political advertisements can be aired briefly, removed from commercial databases, or redistributed without their original labels and surrounding context. Researchers may then be left with transcripts, screenshots, partisan descriptions, or viewer recordings that do not preserve the full experience.

The dispute does not establish that every use of AI in political communication is unlawful. Coverage depends on the enacted definitions, the communication’s content and context, and the evidence available.

## Privacy and civil-liberties significance

Synthetic media can make a real person appear to speak or act in ways that never occurred. In an election context, that can affect identity, reputation, voter understanding, and confidence in authentic recordings.

Disclosure rules may improve transparency while allowing political speech to continue. At the same time, vague or overbroad application could burden satire, illustration, parody, commentary, or ordinary editing. Effective accountability therefore depends on precise definitions, preservation of the original media, visible and accessible labels, clear responsibility rules, and transparent enforcement.

This dispute also demonstrates why provenance matters. A transcript cannot reveal every visual label. A screenshot cannot establish audio content or duration. A repost may crop disclosures. A viewer recording may omit the beginning or end. Without the complete original file and broadcast context, strong claims about compliance or violation are not supportable.

## Lawful actions and resources

- Read the enacted bill text and official legislative history before relying on summaries.
- Treat the July 27 letter as a party allegation, not an official ruling.
- Preserve the complete advertisement, including opening and closing frames, audio, captions, sponsor identification, and platform context, if a lawful copy becomes available.
- Ask AdImpact, the news organizations that reported on the dispute, the relevant broadcasters, and the advertiser whether they can preserve or provide a complete viewer-facing copy.
- Check Tennessee courts, election authorities, the Attorney General, and relevant district attorneys for later complaints, guidance, filings, or decisions.
- When sharing synthetic political media, provide prominent context and avoid stripping labels or presenting the material as authentic.
- Consult a qualified Tennessee election-law or First Amendment attorney for advice about an individual advertisement or legal claim.
