## What happened?

Governor Bill Lee signed Senate Bill 1741 on April 27, 2026. The bill became Public Chapter 831 and is officially titled the **Charlie Kirk Act**.

The Senate adopted Senate Amendment 794 and passed the amended bill 26-6 on March 26. The House passed the final measure 74-22 on April 13. The amendment deleted the introduced bill's operative text and substituted the version that became law.

For purposes of adopting the required policies, the act took effect when it became law. For all other purposes, it took effect July 1, 2026.

The tracker uses **Active** status because the law is in effect and public implementation has begun. Confidence is **Medium** because the statute and several system or institutional policy actions are documented, while the public record does not yet show how every institution handles complaints, investigations, speaker events, demonstrations, student-group recognition, employer access, faculty disputes, records retention, or appeals.

This entry concerns Public Chapter 831. It should not be confused with Public Chapter 701, separately titled the **Charlie Kirk American Heritage Act**, which concerns instruction about religion's influence in American history.

## What the primary source says

### Required freedom-of-expression policy

Each Tennessee public institution of higher education must adopt and publish on its website a freedom-of-expression policy that is identical or substantially similar to the University of Chicago's 2015 Report of the Committee on Freedom of Expression.

The policy must include passages stating, in substance, that:

- universities should not shield people from ideas they find unwelcome, disagreeable, or deeply offensive;
- civility and mutual respect cannot justify closing off discussion;
- institutions may restrict unlawful expression, defamation, genuine threats or harassment, unjustified invasions of substantial privacy or confidentiality interests, expression incompatible with university functions, and expression subject to reasonable time, place, and manner rules;
- debate must not be suppressed merely because ideas are considered offensive, unwise, immoral, or wrong; and
- community members may criticize speakers and ideas but may not obstruct other people's freedom to express them.

The law does not eliminate generally applicable safety rules, harassment law, privacy protections, classroom requirements, or reasonable time, place, and manner regulation. How an institution distinguishes protected criticism from punishable obstruction will depend on the facts, the institution's policy, existing law, and any review or appeal process.

### Required institutional-neutrality policy

Each public institution must also adopt and publish a policy on its role in political and social action that is identical or substantially similar to the University of Chicago's 1967 Kalven Report.

The required passage describes the university as a home and sponsor of individual critics rather than an institutional critic. It emphasizes independence from political pressures and warns that institutional collective positions can inhibit internal dissent.

The act does not define every statement that counts as an institutional position. It does not expressly address every message from a university president, department, academic center, faculty senate, student government, athletics program, official social-media account, emergency office, or diversity and civil-rights office.

### Invited speakers

A public institution, faculty member, or institutional agent may not:

- disinvite a speaker invited by a student organization or faculty member because of the speaker's viewpoint, threatened protests, or opposition from students or faculty;
- require the inviting group or faculty member to disinvite that speaker; or
- prohibit or restrict a student organization or faculty member from inviting a speaker or choosing whom to invite.

The statute does not prescribe a complete event-reservation workflow, security plan, venue-capacity rule, contract form, insurance requirement, or emergency-cancellation standard. It also does not say that an invited speaker is entitled to any requested room, date, subsidy, or security arrangement regardless of neutral operational constraints.

### Academic freedom

A public institution, faculty member, or institutional agent may not retaliate or discriminate against a faculty member because of:

- viewpoints expressed in scholarly work; or
- speech or writing protected by the First Amendment.

The act does not define a special investigation procedure, evidentiary standard, grievance route, remedy, or records-retention period for a faculty claim under this provision.

### Religion and conscience

A public institution, faculty member, or institutional agent may not discriminate or retaliate against a person because of:

- sincere religious beliefs; or
- the person's position concerning abortion, homosexuality, or transgender behavior, whether that position is based on religious or nonreligious beliefs.

The same grounds may not be used to deny recognition to a student group or deny an employer access to on-campus student interviews.

The wording protects a person's **position concerning** the listed subjects. This entry does not interpret it as protecting only one political or religious viewpoint. It also does not treat the provision as permission to engage in otherwise unlawful discrimination, threats, targeted harassment, or conduct outside First Amendment protection.

The statute does not specify how this protection interacts in every circumstance with institutional nondiscrimination policies, federal civil-rights obligations, professional standards, clinical or accreditation rules, or the rights of LGBTQ students and employees.

### Substantial obstruction or interference

Public Chapter 831 expands the statutory examples of conduct that may substantially obstruct or otherwise substantially interfere with another person's expression. The examples include:

- intentionally making noise to drown out an invited speaker or prevent the audience from hearing;
- intentionally standing between a speaker and audience to block their view;
- using signs or objects to block views;
- staging a walkout that results in material and substantial disruption when done with the intent to cause that disruption; and
- physically obstructing a speaker or audience member from entering or attending an event.

The walkout provision contains both an **actual material-and-substantial-disruption requirement** and an **intent requirement**. The act does not state that every silent departure, peaceful protest, critical sign, question, counter-event, or walkout is automatically prohibited.

### What the final law does not create

Public Chapter 831 does not itself establish:

- a statewide campus-speech database;
- a mandatory student or faculty viewpoint registry;
- a new biometric, location-tracking, or identity-verification system;
- a uniform complaint form or investigation portal;
- a statewide list of invited speakers or protest participants;
- a statutory requirement to record every campus event;
- a special data-retention schedule;
- a statewide reporting requirement for complaints or disciplinary outcomes;
- a new private damages action, mandatory statutory damages, or express waiver of sovereign immunity in the final enacted text; or
- a detailed standard for deciding whether a policy is "substantially similar" to the Chicago or Kalven reports.

Earlier descriptions based on the introduced bill may discuss private lawsuits and remedies. Those provisions were not part of the final replacement amendment enacted as Public Chapter 831.

## Documented implementation

### Tennessee Board of Regents

The Tennessee Board of Regents approved several policy changes at its June 12, 2026 board meeting, effective July 1. Its public policy index identifies:

- a revision to Policy 1.03.02.60, **Freedom of Speech and Expression**; and
- new Policy 1.03.02.70, **Institutional Neutrality**.

TBR governs Tennessee's community colleges and Colleges of Applied Technology. The policy action therefore represents system-level implementation across a large portion of the state's public higher-education system.

The public index confirms the policy titles, approval date, and effective date. This project did not obtain a complete institution-by-institution record showing every related handbook, orientation, website, event, student-conduct, human-resources, or training change.

### Volunteer State Community College

Volunteer State Community College states that it adopts applicable TBR policies when TBR creates or modifies them. Its policy index now lists:

- I:01:09, **Freedom of Speech and Expression**; and
- I:01:10, **Institutional Neutrality**.

Vol State's published freedom-of-expression material also illustrates how campus speech policy becomes operational. It addresses event and speaker invitations, outdoor assemblies, activity-fee decisions, security fees, disruption, website publication, handbook publication, annual email, and orientation communications.

Some portions of that freedom-of-expression framework predate Public Chapter 831. Their presence demonstrates the systems and procedures through which implementation may occur; it does not prove every listed rule was newly created by the 2026 act.

### Tennessee State University

Tennessee State University's public policy index, last modified around the law's implementation date, lists a **Freedom of Expression, Campus Speech, and Institutional Neutrality Policy** with the policy number shown as **TBD**.

That public listing is evidence of an implementation workstream or published policy category. It does not, by itself, establish whether every approval, numbering, training, complaint, or technology step was complete when the page was reviewed.

TSU also maintains a campus-free-speech page describing the existing Campus Free Speech Protection Act and linking students to institutional policy and assistance contacts.

### University of Tennessee

The University of Tennessee had related policies before Public Chapter 831, including a system policy on institutional and leadership statements and existing campus free-speech materials.

UT's institutional-statements policy, effective in 2025, generally calls for institutional and leadership neutrality on disputed political, moral, and religious questions while preserving mission-related exceptions and individual expression. UT Knoxville also maintains a public free-speech website.

This review did not locate a clearly labeled Public Chapter 831 revision notice showing which UT policy text, workflow, training, or system configuration changed because of the 2026 act. The existence of earlier related policies may reduce the amount of new policy drafting required, but compliance with the new statute is an institutional legal determination this tracker cannot make.

## Records and systems implicated by implementation

Although the act does not mandate a surveillance platform, implementation can generate or change records in several systems, including:

- policy-management and document-publication systems;
- campus event and room-reservation platforms;
- speaker invitations, contracts, and approval records;
- event-security assessments and staffing plans;
- student-organization recognition and funding systems;
- employer and career-services interview portals;
- protest, disruption, and incident reports;
- student-conduct case-management systems;
- faculty grievance, evaluation, tenure, promotion, and disciplinary records;
- discrimination, retaliation, religious-accommodation, and civil-rights complaint systems;
- official university statement, website, email, and social-media approval workflows;
- annual email, handbook, orientation, and training records;
- video, access-control, or security records created under otherwise applicable campus policies; and
- public-records, litigation-hold, audit, and records-retention systems.

These categories identify where implementation evidence may exist. They do not establish that institutions are collecting every category, monitoring lawful political views, or retaining information beyond existing requirements.

The digital-rights questions concern necessity, proportionality, access, notice, accuracy, retention, appeal rights, and whether records created for event safety or conduct review are later used for unrelated disciplinary, employment, or political purposes.

## What officials say

Legislative supporters describe the act as a protection for open debate, invited speakers, academic freedom, religious conscience, and institutional neutrality. They argue that public colleges should not suppress unpopular viewpoints or allow threatened disruption to determine who may speak.

The law's use of the Chicago free-expression report and Kalven Report reflects a model in which individual students and faculty—not the institution as a corporate body—debate contested political and social questions.

Those statements describe the supporters' policy rationale. They are not independent findings by this tracker that viewpoint discrimination or speaker cancellation occurs at a particular rate, that the required policies will be applied evenly, or that every institutional statement inhibits academic debate.

## What advocates and critics say

Supporters argue that clearer rules against a "heckler's veto" protect speakers across the political spectrum and that institutional neutrality leaves more room for students and faculty to form their own views.

Critics raise several different concerns:

- the statute's explicit reference to positions concerning abortion, homosexuality, and transgender behavior may be used in disputes involving LGBTQ students, faculty, student groups, employers, classroom speech, or campus services;
- broad or inconsistent interpretations of disruption may chill lawful protest, counter-speech, questioning, signs, or walkouts;
- institutional-neutrality rules may make universities reluctant to address discrimination, public emergencies, civil-rights concerns, or issues directly affecting students and employees;
- unclear complaint and investigation practices may lead to selective enforcement; and
- records created through event security, conduct investigations, faculty reviews, or student-group decisions may contain sensitive political, religious, associational, or identity-related information.

The Chicago and Kalven models also have supporters and critics independent of this Tennessee law. The statute requires policies identical or substantially similar to those reports, but it leaves institutions and courts to resolve many boundary questions.

These concerns identify plausible civil-liberties and implementation risks. They do not prove that a particular Tennessee institution has engaged in viewpoint surveillance, discriminatory discipline, unlawful retaliation, or misuse of records.

## What is confirmed?

- Governor Bill Lee signed SB 1741 on April 27, 2026.
- The bill became Public Chapter 831 and is officially titled the Charlie Kirk Act.
- The final Senate vote was 26-6 and the final House vote was 74-22.
- Senate Amendment 794 replaced the introduced bill's operative text.
- The act took effect immediately for policy adoption and July 1, 2026 for all other purposes.
- Each Tennessee public institution of higher education must adopt and publish a freedom-of-expression policy identical or substantially similar to the University of Chicago's 2015 report.
- Each institution must adopt and publish an institutional-role policy identical or substantially similar to the 1967 Kalven Report.
- Institutions may not disinvite or restrict invited speakers for the viewpoint, threatened-protest, or opposition reasons listed in the law.
- The law protects faculty against retaliation or discrimination based on viewpoints in scholarly work and First Amendment-protected speech or writing.
- The law bars discrimination or retaliation based on sincere religious beliefs or a person's position concerning abortion, homosexuality, or transgender behavior.
- The same grounds may not be used to deny student-group recognition or employer access to on-campus student interviews.
- The act expands statutory examples of substantial obstruction or interference, including certain intentional noise, view blocking, materially disruptive intentional walkouts, and physical obstruction.
- The final enacted text does not create the introduced bill's proposed private cause of action or damages structure.
- TBR approved a revised freedom-of-speech policy and a new institutional-neutrality policy on June 12, effective July 1.
- Vol State publicly lists both freedom-of-speech and institutional-neutrality policies.
- TSU's public policy index lists a combined freedom-of-expression, campus-speech, and institutional-neutrality policy with its number shown as TBD when reviewed.
- Public Chapter 831 does not create a statewide speech-monitoring or viewpoint-registration system.

## What remains uncertain?

As of the last review date, the following questions remained unresolved in the public materials reviewed:

- Whether every Tennessee public college and university has formally adopted and published compliant policies
- How each governing board determines that its policy is identical or substantially similar to the Chicago and Kalven reports
- Whether institutions will publish redlined versions showing what changed because of Public Chapter 831
- How institutional-neutrality policies distinguish an official institutional position from academic, administrative, safety, legal, student-support, or mission-related communication
- Which officials and accounts are authorized to speak for an institution
- How colleges will handle statements about discrimination, war, elections, public health, abortion, LGBTQ rights, immigration, or other contested issues
- Which event-reservation and speaker-approval steps have changed
- How institutions distinguish viewpoint-based restrictions from neutral capacity, scheduling, safety, contracting, and funding decisions
- Whether institutions will maintain lists or reports of disinvited, restricted, protested, or disrupted speakers
- What evidence is required to establish intentional drowning out, view blocking, physical obstruction, or a materially disruptive intentional walkout
- How peaceful counter-speech, questions, signs, silent protest, and non-disruptive walkouts will be protected
- Whether campus police or security will create new guidance, training, video-review practices, or incident classifications
- What notice, hearing, evidence, counsel, appeal, and record-correction rights apply in student-conduct cases
- How long event, protest, security, conduct, and complaint records are retained
- Who may access those records and whether access is audited
- Whether records may be reused for employment, admissions, threat assessment, student-group recognition, or unrelated discipline
- How faculty retaliation or discrimination claims will be filed, investigated, and appealed
- How the conscience provision will interact with LGBTQ nondiscrimination rules, Title IX, employment law, classroom management, professional programs, and accreditation
- How student-group recognition and employer-interview decisions will be documented
- Whether institutions will publish aggregate complaint, disciplinary, speaker-event, or appeal statistics
- Whether implementation costs for legal review, training, security, communications, software, and records management will be reported
- Whether litigation will challenge the law or a specific institutional application
- Whether additional statewide guidance will be issued by TBR, the University of Tennessee, the Tennessee Higher Education Commission, the Attorney General, or another state body

## Who may be affected?

Potentially affected people and organizations include:

- students, faculty, staff, applicants, alumni, and campus visitors;
- invited speakers and people organizing protests or counter-events;
- student organizations seeking recognition, funding, rooms, or speakers;
- LGBTQ students, faculty, employees, applicants, and campus organizations;
- religious students, faculty, employees, organizations, and employers;
- people holding any position concerning abortion, homosexuality, or transgender behavior;
- faculty members whose scholarship or public speech addresses disputed subjects;
- employers participating in on-campus interviews;
- campus police, security, event-management, student-affairs, legal, civil-rights, human-resources, and communications staff;
- public-records officers, privacy officers, records managers, system administrators, and software vendors;
- TBR colleges, the University of Tennessee system, locally governed public universities, and their governing boards; and
- journalists, advocates, attorneys, and researchers reviewing campus implementation.

The act can affect people across political and ideological positions. Its practical effect will depend on how institutions apply speech, disruption, neutrality, conscience, nondiscrimination, safety, and due-process rules in specific situations.

## Privacy and civil-liberties significance

Public Chapter 831 is both a speech law and a government-information-governance issue.

Protecting invited speakers and faculty speech can strengthen civil liberties when institutions apply the rules neutrally. Clear limits on obstruction can also help preserve access to events while leaving room for protest and counter-speech.

At the same time, enforcing speech and disruption rules can require institutions to document political expression, religious beliefs, associations, protest activity, event participation, and alleged intent. A student or faculty member may be protected from viewpoint discrimination yet still be affected by how an institution records, labels, shares, and retains an incident or complaint.

Institutional-neutrality rules also create digital governance questions because official positions are communicated through websites, mass email, social media, departmental pages, learning platforms, and other institutional accounts. Determining who speaks for the institution—and preserving approval or revision records—may become part of compliance.

The law does not require viewpoint surveillance or a centralized database. The risk arises if ordinary event, conduct, security, personnel, and communications systems are expanded or linked without clear limits, accurate classification, notice, appeal rights, retention controls, and protection against unrelated secondary use.

The most defensible current conclusion is:

> Public Chapter 831 clearly requires Tennessee public colleges to publish Chicago-style free-expression and Kalven-style institutional-neutrality policies and adds protections involving speakers, faculty speech, conscience, student groups, and employer access. TBR institutions have begun formal policy implementation. The public record does not yet show whether every institution is complete or how complaint, event, protest, discipline, personnel, communications, and record-management systems will apply the law in practice.

## Lawful actions and resources

- Read Public Chapter 831 and Senate Amendment 794 rather than relying on summaries of the introduced bill or confusing it with the Charlie Kirk American Heritage Act.
- Review each public institution's published freedom-of-expression and institutional-neutrality policies.
- Students and employees can ask for the institution's current event, protest, student-conduct, student-organization, faculty-grievance, nondiscrimination, and appeal procedures.
- Researchers can request non-personal public records such as policy drafts, redlines, legal memoranda, board materials, training materials, blank complaint forms, event procedures, records schedules, system-change requests, aggregate complaint statistics, and implementation costs.
- Do not request or publish private student records, personnel files, religious beliefs, political associations, disability information, security vulnerabilities, or identifying details from individual conduct or discrimination cases.
- People involved in an actual discipline, retaliation, discrimination, event, employment, or constitutional dispute should seek qualified individual legal advice rather than relying on this tracker entry.
- Report factual or sourcing problems through the tracker's correction form.
