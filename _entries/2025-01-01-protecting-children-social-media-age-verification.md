## What happened?

The Tennessee General Assembly enacted House Bill 1891, the companion to Senate Bill 2097, as Public Chapter 899 in 2024. Governor Bill Lee signed the measure on May 2, 2024, and it took effect January 1, 2025.

The law is codified at Tennessee Code §§ 47-18-5701 through 47-18-5706 and is titled the **Protecting Children from Social Media Act**.

At its core, the Act requires a covered social media company to verify the age of an individual when that person attempts to become an account holder. If the person is a minor, the company must verify express parental consent before allowing the minor to become an account holder. The company must also allow a parent to revoke consent and must provide parental supervision tools for a minor's account.

The Act prohibits a social media company or third party from retaining personally identifying information used to verify age or parental consent.

NetChoice, a trade association representing internet companies, filed a federal lawsuit on October 3, 2024 challenging the Act under the First and Fourteenth Amendments and the void-for-vagueness doctrine. The law nevertheless took effect January 1, 2025 while the litigation was pending.

The United States District Court for the Middle District of Tennessee denied NetChoice's request for a temporary restraining order on February 14, 2025 and denied its motion for a preliminary injunction on June 18, 2025. The district court concluded that NetChoice had not established irreparable harm and therefore did not proceed through the remaining preliminary-injunction factors.

NetChoice appealed. The Sixth Circuit heard argument on February 4, 2026.

On August 28, 2026, a divided, published Sixth Circuit panel vacated the district court's denial of the preliminary injunction and remanded the case. The majority held that unrecoverable compliance costs can qualify as irreparable harm and that alleged losses of First Amendment freedoms may also constitute irreparable harm when the asserted injury is sufficiently immediate rather than speculative.

The Sixth Circuit did **not** hold the Act unconstitutional, did **not** itself issue an injunction against the law, and did **not** finally resolve NetChoice's First Amendment claims. The district court must now reconsider the preliminary-injunction motion, including the likelihood of success on the merits, the balance of the equities, the public interest, and the scope of any remedy.

## What the primary source says

### Who is covered?

The Act applies to a "social media company," defined through the statute's definition of an interactive computer service that provides a covered "social media platform."

A covered social media platform generally includes a website or internet application that allows a person to create an account and enables account holders to communicate with other account holders and users through posts.

The statute excludes several categories, including broadband internet access services, email services, certain services whose interactive functions are incidental to provider-selected content, certain online-shopping services, career-development services, cloud storage and cloud computing, certain technical-support and product-review services, and peer-to-peer payment platforms.

The Act's account-related requirements concern accounts or profiles created on or after January 1, 2025.

### Age verification for prospective account holders

Tennessee Code § 47-18-5703 requires a social media company to verify the age of an individual when the person attempts to become an account holder.

That means the statutory age-verification obligation is not limited only to people already known to be minors. The company first must determine the age of a prospective account holder in order to determine whether the parental-consent requirement applies.

The Act does not prescribe a single technical age-verification method in the statutory text reviewed for this entry. It does not, on its face, state that every user must upload a government ID, provide a selfie, submit biometric data, use a credit record, or authenticate through a particular identity provider.

Because the statute requires age verification without specifying a universal technical method, the practical privacy burden can depend significantly on how a platform or third-party provider implements that requirement.

### Parental consent for minors

If a prospective account holder is a minor, the social media company must verify the express consent of the minor's parent before allowing the minor to become an account holder.

The Act defines a minor, for these purposes, as an unemancipated Tennessee resident who is known or reasonably believed by a social media platform to be under eighteen years old.

The term "parent" includes a parent, guardian, person with custody, or person with caregiving authority over the minor.

A platform must prohibit a minor from becoming an account holder without the required express parental consent.

Once age and parental consent, when applicable, have been verified, the Act does not require reverification unless parental consent is revoked.

A social media company must allow a parent to revoke consent for a minor to become or continue as an account holder.

### Parental supervision tools

Tennessee Code § 47-18-5704 requires a social media company to provide a minor account holder's parent with means to supervise the minor's account.

At minimum, those tools must allow the parent to:

- view privacy settings on the account;
- set daily time restrictions; and
- implement breaks during which the minor cannot access the account.

The statute establishes these minimum functions but does not, in the statutory language reviewed for this entry, provide a complete technical specification for account linking, parent authentication, custody disputes, delegated caregiving authority, appeal processes, or the handling of incorrect parent-child associations.

### Data retention rule

The Act contains an express privacy safeguard: a social media company or third party may not retain personally identifying information used to verify age or parental consent.

That safeguard is important, but the statute does not itself answer every implementation question. The text reviewed for this entry does not specify a universal technical deletion deadline, independent audit requirement, encryption standard, approved verification vendor, mandatory privacy-preserving credential format, or process for proving that all copies, logs, backups, fraud records, telemetry, and vendor-side data have been deleted.

The existence of a statutory non-retention rule therefore does not by itself establish how much information is collected during verification, where the information travels before deletion, how deletion is validated, or what metadata may be generated by the process.

### Enforcement

The Tennessee Attorney General may investigate and bring enforcement proceedings against a social media company believed to be violating or about to violate the Act when the Attorney General determines that proceedings would be in the public interest.

The Act incorporates enforcement mechanisms from the Tennessee Consumer Protection Act. Depending on the circumstances, those mechanisms can include injunctions, restitution or damages-related relief, civil penalties, recovery of state costs and attorney fees, and other remedies available under the incorporated provisions.

The Sixth Circuit's 2026 opinion described potential civil penalties of up to $1,000 per violation and additional penalties for knowing violations of a court order or injunction.

## Litigation timeline

### October 3, 2024 — NetChoice files suit

NetChoice filed *NetChoice, LLC v. Skrmetti* in the United States District Court for the Middle District of Tennessee, case number 3:24-cv-01191.

NetChoice alleged, among other things, that:

- the Act's coverage rules burden the protected speech of covered services;
- age verification burdens adults' and minors' access to protected speech;
- requiring parental consent before minors may create covered accounts burdens minors' access to protected speech; and
- portions of the Act are unconstitutionally vague.

NetChoice sought declaratory and injunctive relief.

### January 1, 2025 — Act takes effect

The statute took effect while the federal challenge remained pending.

### February 14, 2025 — temporary restraining order denied

After the case was reassigned, the district court denied NetChoice's request for a temporary restraining order.

### June 18, 2025 — preliminary injunction denied

The district court denied NetChoice's motion for a preliminary injunction. Its ruling focused on irreparable harm and concluded that NetChoice had not sufficiently established that factor at that stage.

Because the court treated the irreparable-harm showing as dispositive, it did not complete the remaining preliminary-injunction analysis.

### February 4, 2026 — Sixth Circuit oral argument

The Sixth Circuit heard oral argument in NetChoice's appeal.

Tennessee defended the law as a child-protection and parental-control measure. NetChoice continued to argue that the law burdens protected speech and requires platforms to build costly age-verification and parental-consent infrastructure.

### August 28, 2026 — Sixth Circuit vacates and remands

In a published 2-1 decision, the Sixth Circuit held that the district court's irreparable-harm analysis was legally mistaken.

The majority explained that unrecoverable compliance costs can qualify as irreparable harm and that threatened loss of First Amendment freedoms can also qualify when the alleged injury is sufficiently certain and immediate.

The panel therefore vacated the district court's order denying the preliminary injunction and remanded for further consideration.

The majority expressly left further work for the district court. NetChoice must still establish the other elements relevant to preliminary relief, including likelihood of success on the merits. The district court must also evaluate the balance of equities and public interest and tailor any remedy to the injury actually established.

Judge Clay dissented.

### Current procedural posture

As of August 30, 2026:

- the Protecting Children from Social Media Act remains in effect;
- the Sixth Circuit has vacated the district court's prior denial of preliminary relief;
- the case has been remanded for renewed consideration of NetChoice's preliminary-injunction motion;
- no final judgment on the constitutionality of the Act has been entered in the materials reviewed for this entry; and
- the Sixth Circuit's August 28 decision did not itself enjoin enforcement.

## What officials say

Tennessee officials describe the Act as a child-protection and parental-rights measure intended to address harms they associate with minors' social-media use, including mental-health harms, sleep disruption, cyberbullying, sexual exploitation, and other risks.

Attorney General Jonathan Skrmetti has argued that the law imposes modest requirements on social media companies, gives parents more control over minors' accounts, and does not regulate what users may say online.

Those statements describe Tennessee's policy rationale and litigation position. They do not independently establish how effective the law is, how burdensome implementation is across different platforms, or whether particular verification systems are privacy-preserving.

## What challengers and civil-liberties critics say

NetChoice argues that the law burdens speech by requiring age verification before users may create covered accounts and by preventing minors from creating accounts without verified parental consent.

The challenge also raises questions about whether platforms must collect or process information users would not otherwise need to provide merely to participate in online communities and access lawful expression.

Privacy and civil-liberties concerns include:

- adults may face identity or age checks before creating ordinary social-media accounts;
- users may avoid lawful speech or association rather than participate in verification;
- identity, age, or parental-relationship information may pass through third-party vendors;
- platforms may choose more invasive verification methods than technically necessary;
- systems may misclassify adults as minors or minors as adults;
- parental-consent systems may incorrectly associate a minor with the wrong adult;
- users without conventional identity documents or digital records may face greater friction;
- people with disabilities may encounter inaccessible document, camera, or verification workflows;
- account-level age classification could become reusable beyond the immediate statutory purpose if platforms design systems that way; and
- breaches, misuse, or poor deletion practices could expose sensitive identity or family-relationship information.

The statute's express prohibition on retaining personally identifying information used for verification is a meaningful safeguard against some of these risks. Whether that protection is strong in practice depends on implementation details that are not fully visible from statutory text alone.

People and organizations potentially affected include:

- Tennessee adults creating new social-media accounts;
- Tennessee minors attempting to create new social-media accounts;
- parents, guardians, custodians, and people with caregiving authority;
- social media companies covered by the statute;

Whether any particular service or feature is legally covered can depend on the statutory definitions and facts of that service.

## Privacy and civil-liberties significance

The Act sits at the intersection of child safety, parental authority, anonymous or pseudonymous online participation, identity verification, and First Amendment-protected speech.

The central privacy issue is not simply whether the State requires a person to show a driver's license. The statute does not mandate one universal method. The broader issue is that platforms must establish enough information about a prospective account holder to classify the person by age and, for minors, establish a qualifying adult relationship and verified consent.

That infrastructure can be implemented in more or less privacy-preserving ways.

A lower-data approach could rely on an age-assurance system that returns only a limited result such as "over eighteen" or "under eighteen" without disclosing a name, exact birth date, document number, or persistent identifier to the social-media platform.

A more invasive implementation could collect identity documents, selfies, biometric estimates, transactional information, or persistent account-linked age signals. The statute's non-retention provision constrains retention of personally identifying information used for verification, but it does not itself require the least intrusive available method.

Parental-consent systems add a second layer of identity and relationship verification. A system may need to establish not only that a user is a minor, but that the person granting consent has the legal or caregiving authority recognized by the statute. That creates additional privacy, accuracy, accessibility, and due-process questions.

The First Amendment dispute is also significant because social media is a major forum for reading news, communicating, organizing, participating in communities, publishing creative work, and engaging in political and social discussion. A law that makes account creation conditional on age verification therefore affects access to expressive spaces even though the statute does not directly prescribe what users may say.

At the same time, Tennessee has a substantial policy interest in protecting minors and empowering parents. The unresolved constitutional question is whether the mechanisms chosen by the State impose permissible burdens in pursuit of those interests or burden protected speech more than the Constitution allows.

The Sixth Circuit's August 28, 2026 opinion did not answer that merits question. It instead required the district court to analyze it rather than dispose of the preliminary-injunction request solely on the ground that NetChoice lacked irreparable harm.

The most defensible current conclusion is:

> Tennessee now requires covered social media companies to verify the age of prospective account holders and obtain verified parental consent before allowing minors to open covered accounts. The statute contains an express non-retention rule for personally identifying verification information, but it does not prescribe a single privacy-preserving technical method. The law remains in effect while a federal constitutional challenge continues, and the Sixth Circuit has ordered the district court to reconsider whether preliminary relief should be granted.
