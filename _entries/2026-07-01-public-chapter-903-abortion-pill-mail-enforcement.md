## What happened?

Governor Bill Lee signed Senate Bill 2412 on May 5, 2026. The bill was assigned Public Chapter 903 and took effect July 1, 2026.

The House passed the final amended measure 74-20 on April 16. The Senate passed it 26-6, with one member present and not voting, on April 13. Senate Amendment 1 replaced the introduced bill's operative language and became the enacted text.

Tennessee had already prohibited a manufacturer, supplier, pharmacy, physician, qualified physician, or other person from providing an abortion-inducing drug to a patient by courier, delivery, or mail service. That prohibition was enacted in 2022 as part of the Tennessee Abortion-Inducing Drug Risk Protocol Act and took effect January 1, 2023.

Public Chapter 903 did **not** create the underlying mail restriction. It replaced the attorney general provision in Tennessee Code Annotated § 63-6-1108 with a more specific civil-enforcement and penalty mechanism.

The tracker uses **Active** status because the new enforcement provision is in effect. Confidence is **Medium** because the statute and related Tennessee provisions are documented, while the public record reviewed does not establish how the attorney general will investigate cases, what evidence will be requested, which entities will be targeted, or how out-of-state shield laws will affect access to records and enforcement of Tennessee judgments.

## What the primary source says

### Conduct covered by the new penalty

Public Chapter 903 authorizes the Tennessee Attorney General to bring a civil action to:

- enforce compliance with the Tennessee Abortion-Inducing Drug Risk Protocol Act; or
- impose a civil penalty for a **knowing** violation of Tennessee Code Annotated § 63-6-1103(b).

Section 63-6-1103(b) prohibits a manufacturer, supplier, pharmacy, physician, qualified physician, or other person from providing an abortion-inducing drug to a patient through:

- a courier;
- a delivery service; or
- a mail service.

The statute defines **provide** broadly to include giving, selling, dispensing, administering, transferring possession, otherwise providing, or prescribing an abortion-inducing drug.

### Drugs covered by the statutory definition

Under the existing protocol act, an abortion-inducing drug is a medicine, drug, or substance provided with the intent of terminating a clinically diagnosable pregnancy under the conditions stated in the statute.

The definition includes drugs with abortion-inducing properties, including mifepristone, misoprostol, and methotrexate, when prescribed specifically with the intent of causing an abortion.

The definition excludes drugs prescribed for other medical indications. The same medication may therefore fall inside or outside the act depending on the purpose for which it is prescribed.

### Penalty amounts

The attorney general may generally seek a civil penalty of **$10,000 per violation**.

When a court determines that an individual suffered serious bodily injury or death due to a violation, the law authorizes a civil penalty of **$1,000,000**.

Each individual to whom an abortion-inducing drug is provided constitutes a separate violation.

The enacted provision requires a knowing violation. It does not state that accidental delivery, a mislabeled shipment, or an unknowing intermediary automatically satisfies that standard.

The statute does not fully define:

- what evidence establishes knowledge;
- how causation must be proven for serious bodily injury or death;
- how responsibility is allocated among a prescriber, pharmacy, supplier, platform, fulfillment service, or carrier;
- whether multiple participants may each face a separate penalty for the same patient; or
- how the penalty applies when prescribing, dispensing, fulfillment, shipping, and receipt occur in different states.

### Patient liability under the protocol act

Existing Tennessee law states that a criminal penalty under the protocol act must not be assessed against the patient on whom a chemical abortion is attempted or performed.

It also states that civil liability under the protocol act must not be imposed against that patient.

Those protections are important, but they should not be overstated. This entry concludes only that the protocol act contains patient exemptions from its criminal penalty and civil liability. It does not provide a binding interpretation of every other Tennessee statute or every factual situation.

Legal immunity from liability is also different from privacy. A patient who is not a defendant may still possess information relevant to an action against a provider, pharmacy, supplier, or sender, and records about the patient may be held by third parties.

### Privacy protections in existing private civil actions

The existing protocol act says that, when requested, a court must allow a patient to proceed using initials or a pseudonym in specified private civil actions. The court may also close proceedings and enter other protective orders to preserve patient privacy.

Public Chapter 903 does not expressly state whether the same procedures automatically govern an attorney-general enforcement action. Courts may possess other authority to seal records or issue protective orders, but the new law does not create a specific confidentiality process for attorney-general cases.

### What the law does not create

Public Chapter 903 does not itself establish:

- a patient registry;
- a prescription-reporting database;
- a mandatory report from telehealth providers, pharmacies, or shipping companies;
- a required patient-identification form;
- a data-retention schedule;
- a requirement that platforms preserve accounts or messages;
- a special pre-suit investigative subpoena procedure;
- a patient-notice rule for requests seeking records;
- a required method for redacting or pseudonymizing patient information;
- a rule for sealing attorney-general complaints or evidence;
- a procedure for obtaining records from a shield-law state; or
- a rule resolving conflicts between Tennessee law and another state's law.

The law creates enforcement authority and penalties. The records used in a particular investigation or lawsuit must be established from actual legal demands, pleadings, discovery, court orders, policies, or disclosed practices rather than assumed from the statute alone.

## Records that may become relevant

A mailed telehealth-abortion transaction can create records across several entities and systems. Depending on the facts and legal claims, potentially relevant records could include:

- telehealth registration, intake, consent, and clinical records;
- appointment, provider, and patient-location information;
- prescription and electronic-prescribing records;
- pharmacy dispensing, fulfillment, and inventory records;
- prescription-label and package information;
- shipping labels, addresses, tracking events, delivery confirmations, and carrier metadata;
- payment, billing, refund, and financial-account records;
- email, text-message, portal, and customer-support communications;
- account identifiers, login histories, IP addresses, device information, and location signals;
- provider licensing, employment, and practice records;
- follow-up treatment, emergency-care, laboratory, and adverse-event records; and
- internal compliance, legal-review, audit, and record-access logs.

This is an evidence and data-flow analysis, not a statement that Tennessee has requested every listed category or that every listed record would be discoverable, admissible, accurate, or legally obtainable in a particular case.

### Tennessee civil discovery

Once a civil action is pending, Tennessee's ordinary civil rules allow parties to request documents and electronically stored information within the permitted scope of discovery.

Tennessee Rule of Civil Procedure 45 also permits subpoenas seeking documents and electronically stored information from nonparties. A subpoena recipient may object, and a court may quash or modify an unreasonable or oppressive subpoena or address reasonable production costs.

Public Chapter 903 does not remove those ordinary protections. It also does not explain how Tennessee process would reach a provider, pharmacy, technology company, or record custodian located entirely outside Tennessee.

Personal jurisdiction, service, interstate subpoena procedures, choice of law, federal law, and the responding state's public policy may all matter. The outcome can differ depending on where conduct occurred, where a business is located, where records are stored, which court issued a demand, and which state's court is asked to enforce it.

## Shield laws and interstate conflict

Several states have enacted shield laws intended to protect reproductive-health care that is lawful in the provider's state, including care delivered by telehealth to patients located elsewhere.

### New York

New York's telehealth shield protections are designed to protect New York providers delivering reproductive care to out-of-state patients. New York has also added record-disclosure requirements and restrictions for requests involving legally protected health activity.

Current New York Attorney General guidance says that New York-based people and entities receiving covered subpoenas or information requests generally must notify the Attorney General before disclosure and comply with other shield-law procedures.

New York also allows abortion-medication prescribers to request pharmacy labels that use a practice address instead of the individual provider's name, illustrating how interstate enforcement pressure can change the identifiers placed on physical and electronic records.

A Texas effort to enforce a judgment against a New York telehealth abortion provider produced a direct conflict with New York's shield law in 2025. That dispute demonstrates that interstate judgment and shield-law questions are active legal controversies. It does not decide how a Tennessee Public Chapter 903 case would proceed.

### California

California law generally prohibits covered healthcare entities from releasing abortion-related medical information in response to an out-of-state subpoena or request based on a law that interferes with rights protected in California.

California also regulates access to abortion-related information in electronic health records and health-information exchanges. These protections may matter when a provider, plan, contractor, employer, or data system located in California receives an out-of-state request.

### Massachusetts

Massachusetts treats specified reproductive-health services provided by a Massachusetts-licensed professional physically present in Massachusetts as legally protected when lawful there, regardless of the patient's location.

Massachusetts law provides mechanisms to challenge subpoenas and litigation seeking to punish legally protected care. It also contains protections addressing electronic reproductive-health information.

### Limits of shield-law conclusions

Shield laws differ substantially in their definitions, covered people, records, procedures, exceptions, notice requirements, and remedies.

They do not create a single national rule, and this tracker does not claim that they guarantee anonymity, prevent every investigation, defeat every subpoena, or make every Tennessee judgment unenforceable.

Interstate disputes can involve constitutional questions, personal jurisdiction, full faith and credit, conflicts of law, federal courts, federal agencies, professional licensing, and the exact wording of each state's statute.

## Federal health-privacy context

HIPAA continues to regulate protected health information held by covered entities and business associates, but it is not an absolute shield against every lawful legal demand.

In 2024, the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services adopted additional reproductive-health privacy protections. On June 18, 2025, a federal district court vacated most of that reproductive-health privacy rule. HHS states that only limited notice-of-privacy-practices changes remain in effect.

The result is that providers and record custodians should not assume a special nationwide HIPAA rule automatically blocks every abortion-related request. The legal analysis may depend on ordinary HIPAA provisions, the type of request, state law, patient authorization, court process, and other applicable protections.

This entry does not provide an answer for any specific disclosure request.

## What officials say

Tennessee legislative supporters describe Public Chapter 903 as an enforcement measure for an existing prohibition rather than a new ban.

House sponsor Representative Bryan Terry said the law gives the attorney general a clear mechanism to enforce existing protections. Republican caucus summaries describe the measure as targeting people or organizations that knowingly provide abortion-inducing drugs through prohibited delivery methods.

Supporters argue that higher penalties are necessary to deter out-of-state providers and suppliers and to create accountability when serious injury or death is attributed to a violation.

Those statements describe the sponsors' policy rationale. They are not independent findings by this tracker about the frequency of violations, the safety of telehealth abortion, causation in any individual case, or the legal reach of Tennessee courts beyond the state.

As of the last review date, this project did not locate a clearly labeled Public Chapter 903 enforcement policy, investigative protocol, patient-privacy policy, model complaint, public subpoena, filed enforcement action, aggregate enforcement report, or shield-law guidance from the Tennessee Attorney General's Office.

## What advocates and critics say

Reproductive-rights advocates argue that laws targeting mailed abortion medication create surveillance and privacy risks even when patients are exempt from liability. They point to the possibility that provider records, pharmacy systems, shipping data, payment records, communications, and medical follow-up could be used to identify patients or build cases against out-of-state providers.

Shield-law states argue that providers should be able to deliver care lawful in their state without assisting another state in imposing civil, criminal, or professional penalties. New York, California, and Massachusetts have enacted different combinations of noncooperation, subpoena, disclosure, judgment, licensing, and data-protection measures.

Supporters of Tennessee's law argue that an out-of-state provider should not be able to avoid Tennessee restrictions by using telehealth and mail delivery. They view transaction and delivery records as potential evidence that Tennessee law was knowingly violated.

These positions identify a genuine interstate conflict. They do not establish what records Tennessee will seek, whether a shield-state entity will disclose them, or how a court will resolve a particular demand.

The Society of Family Planning's #WeCount project reports that telehealth accounted for 28% of abortions in the formal U.S. healthcare system during 2025 and that more than half of telehealth abortions were provided under shield laws by December 2025. #WeCount defines telehealth abortion as remote clinician consultation followed by remote dispensing of medication by mail.

Those estimates describe national healthcare activity and include imputed data. They do not identify individual patients, prove a Tennessee-law violation, or establish Tennessee enforcement jurisdiction over a particular provider.

## Privacy and civil-liberties significance

Public Chapter 903 is an enforcement law with significant data implications.

The statute does not require a new surveillance database. Instead, it increases the legal value of records already produced by ordinary healthcare, telehealth, pharmacy, shipping, payment, and communications systems.

To prove a knowing prohibited delivery, an enforcement action may need to connect several facts:

- the identity and location of a patient;
- the identity and location of a prescriber or sender;
- the purpose of the prescription;
- the drug prescribed or dispensed;
- the method and destination of delivery;
- the participants in the transaction; and
- for the higher penalty, the asserted injury and its causal relationship to the violation.

Each fact may reside in a different database controlled by a different entity and governed by a different state's law. Linking those records can expose information not only about a defendant, but also about a patient whom the protocol act says should not bear civil or criminal liability.

The interstate shield-law conflict creates a second layer of digital-rights concern. Tennessee may view records as evidence of conduct affecting a Tennessee patient, while a provider's home state may classify the same care and records as legally protected activity that must not be disclosed or used to impose out-of-state penalties.

The most defensible current conclusion is:

> Public Chapter 903 clearly adds substantial attorney-general penalties for knowing violations of Tennessee's existing mail-delivery prohibition. It does not create a patient database or directly impose liability on patients under the protocol act. The public record does not yet show what records Tennessee will seek, how patients will be protected during enforcement, or how courts will resolve conflicts with shield-law states that restrict disclosure and cooperation.

## Lawful actions and resources

- Read Public Chapter 903 together with the complete Tennessee Abortion-Inducing Drug Risk Protocol Act rather than treating the 2026 law as the source of the original mail prohibition.
- Watch the Tennessee Attorney General, state court dockets, and Tennessee legislative materials for enforcement actions, subpoenas, settlements, guidance, or amendments.
- Researchers can request non-patient public records such as enforcement policies, legal memoranda, blank subpoena templates, aggregate case statistics, contracts, data-retention policies, and communications with other government agencies.
- Do not request, publish, or attempt to identify an individual patient's medical records, prescription, address, shipping history, payment information, communications, account data, or pregnancy outcome.
- Organizations receiving legal demands should obtain qualified counsel in every relevant jurisdiction before disclosing protected records.
- Patients and providers facing an actual investigation, subpoena, disclosure question, or legal threat should seek individual legal advice rather than relying on this tracker entry.
