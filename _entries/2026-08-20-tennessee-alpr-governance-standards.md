## Research and legal limitations

This entry is public-record research, not legal advice. It distinguishes statewide proposals, current Tennessee law, TBI's own public statements, vendor platform rules, local-agency examples, and facts that remain unknown.

The public record reviewed for this entry does **not** establish TBI's complete ALPR architecture, the identities or number of individual TBI users, the exact search fields required by TBI policy, the frequency of TBI audits, or whether TBI has Flock Statewide Lookup, Flock National Lookup, or a Motorola/Vigilant account. The absence of a public document does not prove that a policy, restriction, account, or safeguard does not exist.

Vendor capabilities are not treated as proof that every Tennessee agency has enabled those capabilities. Local police policies are used only as comparators and are not generalized to TBI.

## What happened?

On August 20, 2026, the **Tennessee Sheriffs' Association (TSA)** and **Tennessee Association of Chiefs of Police (TACP)** announced that they were developing recommended principles and model-policy guidance for law-enforcement use of automated license plate reader technology in Tennessee.

The associations said they expect to present recommendations to the Tennessee General Assembly before the 2027 legislative session. Their joint release proposes safeguards covering search purpose, human verification, historical searches, retention and deletion, user access, audits, information sharing, hot-list accuracy, constitutionally protected activity, public complaints, and consequences for intentional misuse.

One day later, the **Tennessee Bureau of Investigation (TBI)** published its own statement saying that it already uses ALPR information in select investigations for documented public-safety purposes. TBI said access is limited to trained and authorized personnel, several categories of unauthorized use are prohibited, audit records are maintained, and ALPR information is treated as an investigative lead rather than conclusive evidence.

The TBI statement gives interstate examples. It says ALPR information in Georgia helped narrow the search for a one-year-old taken from Tennessee, and ALPR information helped investigators track a missing at-risk Tennessee adult's vehicle to Colorado. It also says LPR data helped focus TBI's investigation after a Tennessee state trooper was shot in 2024.

Separate public Flock Safety transparency pages for multiple Tennessee agencies list **Tennessee Bureau of Investigation** in their sharing networks. The pages reviewed for this entry include Mt. Juliet Police Department, Piperton Police Department, and Washington County Sheriff's Office.

That is significant because it moves one part of TBI's access architecture from inference to documentation: at least some Tennessee Flock agencies have configured their data to be shared with TBI. It does **not** establish that TBI can search every Flock camera in Tennessee or nationwide.

## What the primary source says

### TSA/TACP's proposed safeguards

The August 20 joint release says the associations are considering safeguards that would:

- require a documented, legitimate law-enforcement purpose for ALPR use and searches;
- treat an ALPR alert as an investigative lead rather than proof of guilt and require human verification before enforcement action;
- establish controls for historical searches, retention, and deletion;
- limit access to trained, authorized personnel and audit searches and system access;
- establish rules for information sharing and accurate alert and hot-list information;
- protect lawful political, religious, journalistic, associational, and other constitutionally protected activity from inappropriate monitoring or use; and
- provide meaningful processes for public concerns and complaints and accountability for intentional misuse.

The release explicitly describes those safeguards as **prospective standards under consideration**, not as rules that already bind every Tennessee agency.

It also says serious or intentional misconduct should be subject to meaningful discipline, potentially including termination when warranted, and that accountability should extend to supervisors or agency leaders who knowingly permit serious misuse or fail to act after discovering violations.

### TBI's current public safeguard statement

TBI's August 21 newsroom statement says it uses ALPR information only in select investigations for documented, lawful public-safety purposes. TBI says it:

- limits access to trained, authorized personnel;
- prohibits personal, political, retaliatory, discriminatory, commercial, and other unauthorized uses;
- has policy governing authorized uses, training, access, security, information sharing, retention, auditing, and consequences for misuse;
- treats ALPR information as investigative leads rather than conclusions; and
- maintains audit records and conducts reviews for appropriate use, data accuracy, retention compliance, and continued public-safety value.

The statement is strong evidence of what TBI says its internal safeguards cover. It is **not the internal policy itself**. It does not disclose whether a TBI search requires a case number, incident number, offense category, narrative reason, supervisor approval, or another specific field, and it does not disclose the Bureau's audit schedule or sampling method.

### Current Tennessee law

Tennessee Code § 55-10-302 generally limits governmental retention of captured ALPR plate data to **90 days**, unless the data are retained as part of an ongoing investigation. When investigative data are retained under that exception, the statute requires destruction at the conclusion of an investigation that produces no charges or at the conclusion of the resulting criminal action.

Tennessee's public-records law also treats captured plate data as confidential and not open for ordinary public inspection. The confidentiality provision previously contained a July 1, 2026 expiration date. **Public Chapter 672 / SB 1879-HB 1642**, signed April 7, 2026, removed that expiration.

For fixed ALPR cameras installed on state highway rights-of-way, Tennessee also has an additional review process under T.C.A. § 55-8-198 and the Tennessee Department of Transportation application procedure. The current TDOT application requires a written law-enforcement ALPR policy containing provisions for **data privacy, records retention, and audit logs**. The process also requires security and manufacturer documentation and provides for Tennessee Department of Safety and Homeland Security review before final TDOT approval.

These requirements create an existing state-level policy floor for covered right-of-way installations. They do not establish the full operational rules used by TBI or by every local ALPR system.

### Flock sharing and search controls

Flock's public law-enforcement documentation says each user has an individual authenticated account, shared logins are not permitted, searches must be tied to an investigative purpose, every search is recorded and linked to the user, supervisors can review search history, and each customer agency decides whether to share its data.

Flock also says its ALPR search interface now requires an **Offense Type** for every search, including searches of an agency's own cameras, Statewide Lookup, and National Lookup. Search Reason can provide additional detail, and agencies can configure requirements around that field.

Those are current vendor-level controls. They do not reveal TBI's internal policy, user roster, account roles, or audit frequency.

### Documented TBI access to local Flock data

Public Flock transparency pages reviewed for this entry include TBI in the sharing networks of:

- Mt. Juliet Police Department;
- Piperton Police Department; and
- Washington County Sheriff's Office.

The most defensible conclusion is that TBI has documented organizational access to data shared by at least those agencies through Flock's permission-based sharing system.

This evidence does **not** establish that TBI participates in Flock's separate Statewide Lookup or National Lookup products. Flock describes National Lookup as an opt-in network through which participating police departments may search a full, known plate against cameras other participating departments have chosen to share with that network. No Tennessee-specific public record reviewed for this entry states that TBI has that capability.

### A separate statewide Motorola/Vigilant procurement system

Tennessee also maintains **Statewide Contract 450 / Edison 69792** with Motorola Solutions for license plate recognition systems. June 2026 Fiscal Review material describes fixed and mobile ALPR hardware, software, installation, training, maintenance, and support; lists the system expiration date as May 16, 2027; and reports approximately **$1.92 million** in spending through May 27, 2026.

The same material identifies the main user categories as **Safety, Correction, and Mental Health**.

This establishes a statewide Motorola ALPR procurement pathway. It does **not** establish that TBI purchases or uses Motorola/Vigilant ALPR through that contract. A targeted public-source search did not locate a TBI purchase order, invoice, account record, or usage report under SWC 450.

That is a negative public-source finding, not proof that no direct or indirect TBI access exists.

## What officials say

TSA and TACP say ALPR can help locate stolen vehicles, missing and endangered people, wanted persons, and vehicles connected to serious investigations, but they also say the ability to collect, search, retain, and share location information requires clearer safeguards and accountability.

TBI Director David Rausch described ALPR as useful when used properly and purposefully, while saying technology this powerful requires clear rules and meaningful accountability.

The International Association of Chiefs of Police issued a separate statement on August 20 supporting ALPR for legitimate public-safety purposes while recommending documented lawful purposes, trained users, comprehensive written policies, human verification, tamper-resistant audit records, regular audits, cybersecurity controls, restricted sharing, and consequences for misuse. TBI's statement says its safeguards are consistent with IACP best practices.

The timing and similarities make IACP's statement an important national comparator. The available evidence does **not** establish that IACP drafted Tennessee's proposal or that vendors supplied the language.

## What advocates and critics say

The Electronic Frontier Foundation describes ALPR databases as a location-privacy risk because a large number of time-and-place observations can be searched across jurisdictions and may reveal visits to homes, workplaces, healthcare facilities, religious institutions, political events, and other sensitive locations.

Critics of networked ALPR systems have also raised concerns about broad interagency sharing, searches unrelated to legitimate investigations, incorrect plate or hot-list information, and weak review of audit logs.

Those concerns help explain why the TSA/TACP proposal specifically addresses historical searches, sharing, audits, protected activity, complaint processes, and discipline. They are **not findings that TBI or the Tennessee agencies named in this entry have committed misuse**.

This review did not locate a substantiated Tennessee-specific public case establishing intentional TBI ALPR misuse.

## What is confirmed?

- TSA and TACP announced a joint ALPR policy initiative on August 20, 2026.
- They expect to present recommendations to the General Assembly before the 2027 legislative session.
- Their proposed safeguards include documented search purpose, human verification, access restrictions, audits, sharing rules, protected-activity language, complaint processes, and consequences for intentional misuse.
- The associations explicitly describe the safeguards as prospective standards under consideration.
- TBI says it currently uses ALPR information in select investigations for documented public-safety purposes.
- TBI says access is limited to trained and authorized personnel, unauthorized uses are prohibited, policy governs retention and sharing, audit records are maintained, and ALPR information is treated as an investigative lead.
- TBI's own examples show ALPR-derived investigative information crossing state lines in Georgia and Colorado cases involving people from Tennessee.
- Public Flock transparency pages for Mt. Juliet, Piperton, and Washington County list TBI in their sharing networks.
- Flock says sharing is permission-based, individual users are authenticated, and searches are logged and reviewable.
- Tennessee generally limits captured ALPR plate-data retention by governmental entities to 90 days unless an ongoing-investigation exception applies.
- Tennessee's captured-plate confidentiality provision remains in effect after Public Chapter 672 removed its former July 2026 expiration.
- TDOT's current right-of-way application requires covered agencies to submit written ALPR policies addressing privacy, retention, and audit logs.
- Tennessee has a statewide Motorola ALPR contract, SWC 450 / Edison 69792, extended through May 16, 2027.

## Who may be affected?

ALPR systems can create searchable time-and-location observations about vehicles driven by residents, commuters, visitors, crime victims, witnesses, suspects, and people not suspected of wrongdoing.

People who may be affected include:

- Tennessee drivers and registered vehicle owners;
- passengers whose presence may be inferred from other evidence;
- people driving rented, borrowed, family-owned, or employer-owned vehicles;
- people traveling across city, county, or state boundaries;
- people whose vehicles are incorrectly associated with a misread, cloned, stolen, transferred, or outdated plate;
- missing or endangered people and crime victims who may benefit from faster vehicle identification; and
- law-enforcement personnel and agencies whose access is governed by sharing, search, and audit rules.

A plate observation does not by itself establish who was driving, who else was present, the purpose of a trip, or whether unlawful activity occurred.

## Privacy and civil-liberties significance

The central digital-rights issue is not merely that cameras photograph visible license plates. It is that many timestamped observations can become a **searchable record of vehicle movement**, and those records may be shared across agencies and jurisdictions.

The August 2026 development is notable because Tennessee law-enforcement organizations themselves are now proposing stronger standards around exactly those questions: who may search, why a search may be run, what happens after an automated alert, how historical data are retained, who receives shared data, how lawful activity is protected, how searches are audited, and what happens when someone misuses the system.

TBI's disclosure makes the debate operational rather than hypothetical. The Bureau already uses ALPR information, and multiple Tennessee Flock agencies publicly list TBI in their sharing networks.

At the same time, the public record is incomplete enough that the Tracker cannot yet evaluate whether TBI's internal rules are as specific as the proposed TSA/TACP standards. TBI says its policy covers access, retention, sharing, audits, and misuse consequences, but the actual policy and audit procedures were not located publicly during this review.

Tennessee's existing 90-day retention limit, confidentiality rule, and TDOT/TDOSHS right-of-way process are meaningful safeguards. The new TSA/TACP initiative could add more specific operational expectations, particularly around documented search purposes, human verification, audit practices, protected activity, complaints, and discipline. Whether those expectations become law, binding statewide policy, accreditation requirements, grant conditions, or voluntary guidance remains unresolved.

The most defensible current conclusion is:

> Tennessee's sheriffs' and police-chiefs' associations are developing statewide ALPR safeguards while TBI already uses ALPR information and is listed in multiple local Flock sharing networks. The public record establishes some platform and state-law safeguards but does not yet reveal TBI's detailed internal search, sharing, or audit rules or the full breadth of its network access.

## Lawful actions and resources

- Read the TSA/TACP joint release and TBI's August 21 newsroom statement.
- Review Tennessee's 90-day retention rule and the 2026 law preserving confidentiality of captured plate data.
- Review the TDOT right-of-way application to see the privacy, retention, and audit-log policy requirements that apply to covered installations.
- Review local ALPR policies, such as Johnson City's published policy, as examples of safeguards Tennessee agencies can make public without disclosing individual plate histories.
- Follow the Tennessee General Assembly for the TSA/TACP recommendations or resulting legislation before the 2027 session.
- Ask state and local agencies to publish non-investigative governance information such as written ALPR policies, sharing categories, aggregate user counts, audit methods, aggregate compliance findings, and complaint procedures.
- Ask local governing bodies to review ALPR policy, sharing, audit, and renewal terms when contracts are renewed or systems expand.
- Avoid publishing individual plate histories, personally identifying travel records, sensitive camera coordinates, credentials, or active-investigation information.
- For an individual legal question involving ALPR evidence, privacy rights, or a law-enforcement encounter, consult a qualified Tennessee attorney.
