## Research and legal limitations

This entry is public-record research, not legal advice. The interaction among Tennessee law, HIPAA, federal substance-use-disorder confidentiality rules, Title X family-planning requirements, professional duties, court orders, and the facts of an individual case can be complex.

The project and its contributors:

- cannot determine from public sources whether a particular parent or other person is legally entitled to a specific minor’s records;
- cannot independently verify nonpublic electronic-health-record configurations, patient-portal permissions, safety flags, audit logs, or internal legal guidance;
- do not claim that Public Chapter 883 overrides every federal confidentiality rule; and
- recommend review by a qualified Tennessee attorney or health-privacy professional before drawing especially strong conclusions about an individual disclosure.

Unknowns are identified below rather than filled with assumptions.

## What happened?

Governor Bill Lee signed Senate Bill 259 on May 5, 2026. The measure was later assigned Public Chapter 883 by the Tennessee Secretary of State. The act took effect upon becoming law.

The final legislation differs materially from the bill as introduced. On April 14, 2026, the Senate adopted Senate Amendment 2, which deleted all language after the enacting clause and substituted the text that became law. The House then substituted the Senate bill and passed it on April 16.

The enacted law makes several related changes concerning unemancipated minors’ medical records:

1. It expands access to prescription and rehabilitation records connected with certain mental-health treatment available to older minors without parental consent.
2. It states that access rights operate only to the extent allowable under federal privacy laws and regulations.
3. It places the right to access a child’s medical records, including defined prescription and rehabilitation records, in Tennessee’s Families’ Rights and Responsibilities Act.
4. It requires providers and facilities to provide access to medical records from several categories of treatment that Tennessee law permits minors to receive without parental consent.
5. It retains an exception tied to mandatory abuse reporting and danger to the minor’s life or physical safety.
6. It requires parental notification when a treating professional determines, under the statute’s clinical threshold, that a minor who communicated suicidal ideation has the apparent ability and likelihood to attempt suicide unless prevented.

This entry focuses on record access, confidentiality, and electronic implementation. It does not analyze the separate bleeding-control-kit provision added by section 5 of the act.

## What the primary source says

### Prescription and rehabilitation records under Tennessee Code § 33-8-202

Tennessee law allows certain minors age 16 or older who have a serious emotional disturbance or mental illness to receive specified outpatient treatment or rehabilitation services without parental consent.

Public Chapter 883 provides that, to the extent allowable under federal privacy laws and regulations, a parent, legal guardian, or legal custodian may access prescription and rehabilitation records resulting from treatment under that section.

The law defines **prescription records** broadly. The definition includes documents, data, or other recorded information created, maintained, or transmitted by a licensed provider, pharmacy, or other authorized entity concerning the prescribing, dispensing, or administration of medication. Examples listed in the statute include:

- written, electronic, or verbal prescriptions;
- medication orders;
- dosage instructions;
- refill authorizations;
- dispensing logs;
- pharmacy records; and
- prescription modifications, renewals, or cancellations.

The law defines **rehabilitation records** as records concerning a diagnosis, treatment recommendation, discharge summary, or prescribed course of action connected with rehabilitation services.

The definition excludes certain personal notes, statements, or communications originating from the patient that are documented, interpreted, or formalized by the provider as part of the medical record. The exact boundary of this exclusion—particularly for progress notes and mixed records containing both patient statements and provider analysis—is not fully explained in the statute.

### Access to records from care provided without parental consent

Public Chapter 883 also amends Tennessee Code § 68-11-304. A parent, legal guardian, legal custodian, or other person with medical decision-making authority for an unemancipated minor may access, and a healthcare provider or facility must provide access to, medical records resulting from treatment provided without parental consent under listed statutes.

Those statutes concern:

| Tennessee statute | General subject of the care |
|---|---|
| § 68-10-104(c) | Examination, diagnosis, and treatment for sexually transmitted infections without parental knowledge or consent |
| § 68-34-107 | Contraceptive supplies and information for minors who meet the statute’s eligibility provisions |
| § 63-6-220 | Physician treatment of juvenile drug abuse without prior parental consent |
| § 63-6-222 | Emergency medical or surgical treatment when delaying for consent would endanger the minor or worsen the condition |
| § 63-6-223 | Prenatal and qualifying peripartum care without parental consent |

The referenced medical-record definition in Tennessee Code § 63-2-101 includes medical histories, records, reports and summaries, diagnoses, prognoses, treatment and medication records, radiology interpretations, physical-therapy notes, and laboratory reports.

Public Chapter 883 expands access to the **medical records** arising from the listed care. It should not be described merely as a pharmacy-record law.

### Safety exception

The law prohibits a parent, legal guardian, or legal custodian from accessing the covered records when both of the following conditions are met:

1. The treating professional is required to report abuse under Tennessee Code § 37-1-403 or § 37-1-605; and
2. The professional believes disclosure is reasonably likely to endanger the minor’s life or physical safety.

This is a real statutory protection, but it is written narrowly. The text requires an abuse-reporting duty and a danger determination. It does not expressly explain whether the state-law exception applies in other endangerment situations that do not also trigger one of the listed abuse-reporting statutes.

The affirmative access provision in § 68-11-304(i) includes an “other person with medical decision-making authority.” The safety exception in subsection (j), however, expressly names a parent, legal guardian, or legal custodian. The practical effect of that difference is not explained in the act.

### Suicidal-ideation notification

Under the amended mental-health provision, a treating professional must notify the parent, guardian, or custodian when:

- the unemancipated minor communicates suicidal ideation; and
- applying the professional standard stated in the law, the professional determined or reasonably should have determined that the minor has the apparent ability to attempt suicide and is likely to do so unless prevented.

The statute does not require notification based on every reference to suicidal thoughts. It uses the more specific ability-and-likelihood threshold described above.

### Federal-law limitation

The enacted text expressly states that the access provisions operate only “to the extent allowable by federal privacy laws and regulations.” That qualification is important because different federal rules can apply to different providers and records.

#### HIPAA

HHS guidance explains that parents are generally personal representatives of their minor children under the HIPAA Privacy Rule. HIPAA contains exceptions when a minor can independently consent to care, but it also defers to state laws that expressly require, permit, or prohibit parental access.

As a result, HIPAA does not automatically make all minor-consented care confidential from a parent when a state has enacted an express access rule. At the same time, HIPAA allows a covered entity to decline to treat a person as a personal representative when the entity reasonably believes that doing so could endanger the individual and, using professional judgment, concludes that declining access is in the individual’s best interests.

How that federal discretion interacts with Tennessee’s more specifically worded mandatory safety exception may depend on the provider, record, circumstances, and legal interpretation.

#### Federally protected substance-use-disorder records

Records held by a federally assisted substance-use-disorder program may be subject to 42 CFR Part 2 in addition to HIPAA.

Under 42 CFR § 2.14, when state law allows a minor acting alone to obtain substance-use-disorder treatment, consent for a covered use or disclosure may be given only by the minor. The regulation expressly includes disclosure of patient-identifying information to a parent or guardian for reimbursement purposes.

Because Tennessee Code § 63-6-220 allows physicians to treat juvenile drug abuse without prior parental consent, Part 2 may substantially limit or prevent disclosure by a covered Part 2 program even though Public Chapter 883 generally directs access. Not every provider or rehabilitation record is covered by Part 2, and determining coverage requires program-specific facts.

#### Title X family-planning records

Current Title X regulations require projects to keep personal information confidential and prohibit project staff from notifying a parent before or after a minor requests or receives Title X services. The same regulation permits disclosure when required by law, with appropriate confidentiality safeguards.

Public Chapter 883 therefore creates a legal implementation question for Title X providers rather than a simple conclusion that all Title X records must always be disclosed or must always remain inaccessible. This entry does not provide a binding interpretation of that interaction.

## What officials say

A Tennessee House Republican Caucus statement describing the legislation said it was intended to clarify and strengthen parental access, align state law with the Families’ Rights and Responsibilities Act, eliminate conflicting interpretations, and create greater uniformity in how prescription and rehabilitation information is categorized and accessed.

The statement also identified the abuse-and-safety restriction as a safeguard for vulnerable minors and described the suicidal-ideation provision as a means of informing families during critical situations.

These statements explain the sponsor’s rationale. They do not answer how providers must configure patient portals, separate protected records, resolve competing federal rules, or handle contested access requests.

## What advocates and critics say

The Tennessee Primary Care Association, which represents community health centers, published an implementation explanation in June 2026. It interpreted the law as applying to records concerning sexually transmitted infection services and contraception, subject to the safety exception.

The association said the statute does not expressly require providers to change release forms, but recommended updating forms or giving patients a separate notice that parents or guardians may request covered records. It also stated that it was seeking clarification about whether progress notes fall within the law.

As of the last review date, this project did not locate a detailed, publicly posted analysis of the final enacted text from a Tennessee civil-liberties, reproductive-rights, adolescent-health, or LGBTQ-rights organization. The absence of a located statement should not be interpreted as support, opposition, or proof that no such analysis exists.

The House and Senate vote records show legislative opposition, but votes alone do not establish each lawmaker’s reasoning.

## What is confirmed?

- Public Chapter 883 was signed on May 5, 2026, and took effect upon becoming law.
- Senate Amendment 2 replaced the earlier substantive bill language and became the controlling enacted text.
- The law expands access to prescription and rehabilitation records arising from specified mental-health treatment for unemancipated minors.
- The law expands access from prescription records to medical records arising from listed categories of treatment that minors may receive without parental consent.
- The listed categories include sexually transmitted infection services, contraceptive services, juvenile drug-abuse treatment, emergency treatment, and prenatal or qualifying peripartum care.
- The affirmative access provision covers parents, legal guardians, legal custodians, and other persons with medical decision-making authority.
- The state-law safety exception applies when an abuse-reporting duty exists and the treating professional believes access is reasonably likely to endanger the minor’s life or physical safety.
- The act expressly conditions access on federal privacy laws and regulations.
- HIPAA generally defers to express state rules governing parental access while retaining an abuse, neglect, and endangerment provision.
- 42 CFR Part 2 contains stricter rules for covered substance-use-disorder records when a minor may independently obtain treatment.
- The Tennessee Primary Care Association advised providers that the law reaches sexually transmitted infection and contraceptive records and recommended patient notice, while acknowledging unresolved questions about progress notes.
- The final law did not enact the introduced bill’s broader proposal requiring parental consent for essentially all medical treatment of minors.

## What remains uncertain?

As of July 28, 2026, this project did not locate publicly posted statewide implementation guidance, a model access form, a patient-portal standard, or detailed technical instructions from the Tennessee Department of Health, Department of Mental Health and Substance Abuse Services, TennCare, or a Tennessee health-profession licensing board.

Important unresolved questions include:

- Whether covered records become automatically visible to an existing parental proxy account or remain available only through a reviewed request
- Whether providers will change access prospectively or expose older records already held in an electronic system
- How providers will authenticate a parent, guardian, custodian, or other medical decision-maker
- How electronic systems will separate Part 2 records, Title X records, psychotherapy notes, patient-originated statements, and other specially protected information
- Whether progress notes, mixed clinical notes, messaging threads, and after-visit summaries fall within or outside the rehabilitation-record exclusion
- Whether the exclusion for patient-originated statements can be implemented reliably when those statements are embedded within provider-authored notes
- How a clinician documents the abuse-and-safety exception without creating an alert visible to the person from whom records are being withheld
- Whether a portal block, disclosure, download, or access denial is logged and available for later audit
- What notice is given to minors before or after records are provided
- What appeal, review, or correction process applies when access is granted or denied incorrectly
- How the safety exception applies to an “other person with medical decision-making authority,” given the difference between the wording of the access and exception provisions
- When a rehabilitation provider qualifies as a federally assisted Part 2 program
- How Title X projects reconcile the state access mandate with federal confidentiality and anti-notification requirements
- Whether providers or pharmacies will charge copying or processing fees and what response deadlines apply to different forms of access
- Whether litigation, regulatory guidance, or attorney-general opinions will clarify any of these questions

The absence of publicly posted instructions does not prove that providers or agencies lack internal policies or legal guidance.

## Who may be affected?

The law may directly affect:

- unemancipated minors receiving mental-health treatment or rehabilitation services under Tennessee Code § 33-8-202;
- minors receiving sexually transmitted infection examination, diagnosis, or treatment;
- minors receiving contraceptive information or supplies under Tennessee law;
- minors receiving treatment for drug abuse without prior parental consent;
- minors receiving emergency medical or surgical treatment;
- minors receiving prenatal or qualifying peripartum care;
- parents, guardians, custodians, and other legally authorized medical decision-makers;
- hospitals, clinics, community health centers, mental-health professionals, pharmacies, rehabilitation providers, and Title X or Part 2 programs; and
- health-information-technology staff responsible for proxy access, privacy segmentation, record release, and audit logging.

The statute is not written specifically as an LGBTQ or transgender law. LGBTQ minors may nevertheless be affected when they use one of the covered categories of care, particularly mental-health, sexual-health, contraceptive, or substance-use services. That possible impact should not be expanded beyond the law’s actual coverage without additional evidence.

## Privacy and civil-liberties significance

Public Chapter 883 does not create a government medical database or authorize public disclosure of minors’ records. Its digital-rights significance comes from changing the legal boundary between a minor’s confidential care and an authorized adult’s access to information held by providers, pharmacies, facilities, and electronic systems.

That boundary matters because modern medical records may be distributed across:

- patient and proxy portals;
- electronic-health-record notes and results;
- pharmacy dispensing systems;
- electronic prescriptions;
- billing and claims systems;
- health-information exchanges;
- secure messages and appointment summaries; and
- specialized mental-health, rehabilitation, Title X, or substance-use systems.

A rule that appears straightforward on paper can be difficult to implement safely when one record contains information subject to different consent, disclosure, and safety rules. Automatic proxy access may create different risks from a manually reviewed records request, while overly broad blocking can improperly deny lawful access.

The most defensible current conclusion is:

> Public Chapter 883 expands access to sensitive records from care that Tennessee permits some minors to receive without parental consent, but the act does not establish one universal disclosure rule for every provider or record. Federal program rules, safety determinations, record type, legal authority, and the design of electronic access systems can materially change the result.

Supporters describe the law as promoting parental involvement, consistency, and transparency. Privacy concerns center on whether minors understand that care they can legally obtain without parental consent may generate records accessible to a parent or other adult, and whether providers can correctly prevent disclosures barred by federal law or presenting a safety risk.

Those concerns cannot be resolved from statutory text alone. Implementation evidence, technical policies, and qualified legal review are necessary.

## Lawful actions and resources

- Read Public Chapter 883 and the adopted Senate amendment rather than relying on summaries of the introduced bill.
- Ask a healthcare provider for its written policy on adolescent confidentiality, parental or proxy access, and specially protected records.
- Before receiving care, ask what information will appear in a patient portal, proxy account, explanation of benefits, pharmacy account, or after-visit summary.
- Ask whether the provider gives minors a notice explaining the circumstances in which records may be released to a parent or guardian.
- Ask the Tennessee Department of Health, Department of Mental Health and Substance Abuse Services, relevant licensing boards, and TennCare to publish implementation guidance, model notices, and frequently asked questions.
- Request non-patient-level public records concerning agency legal guidance, provider bulletins, model forms, training materials, communications with regulated entities, and technical access requirements. Do not request identifiable medical records involving minors.
- Ask providers how they identify and segregate records protected by 42 CFR Part 2, Title X, HIPAA psychotherapy-note rules, or other specialized confidentiality laws.
- Submit factual corrections or newly released primary sources through this repository’s public issue forms.
- For questions involving personal access, confidentiality, safety, custody, or treatment decisions, consult a qualified Tennessee attorney, healthcare professional, or established patient-rights organization.
