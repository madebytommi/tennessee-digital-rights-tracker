## What happened?

Governor Bill Lee signed House Bill 1271 on May 7, 2026. It was assigned Public Chapter 938 on May 15.

The House passed the final amended bill 72-16 on April 20, 2026. The Senate passed the House bill 24-6 on April 22. The adopted House amendment replaced the introduced bill's original one-line substance and became the operative enacted text.

Public Chapter 938 creates a statewide mandate governing how state and local governmental entities define **sex** or **gender** in ordinances, resolutions, rules, policies, and procedures.

The law took effect when signed. It establishes two additional implementation dates:

- Beginning July 1, 2026, covered entities may not adopt new covered materials using a definition inconsistent with the statute.
- By January 1, 2027, covered entities must revise existing covered materials that use the terms sex or gender.

The tracker uses **Active** status because the law is in effect and its July 1 restriction has begun. Confidence is **Medium** because the enacted requirements are documented, while the public record reviewed does not fully establish how agencies and local governments will modify forms, databases, portals, identity workflows, historical records, or data exchanges before January 1, 2027.

## What the primary source says

### Required definition

When a covered ordinance, resolution, rule, policy, or procedure refers to a natural person's sex or gender, the governmental entity must define those terms as:

> the immutable characteristics of the reproductive system that identify the person as male or female, as determined by anatomy and genetics existing at the time of birth.

The enacted text does **not** direct people to undergo genetic testing. The phrase describes the statutory definition; it does not establish an individual testing procedure, proof requirement, or medical examination.

The law uses the same substantive definition for state and local governmental entities.

### Local governmental entities

The local provisions apply broadly to:

- counties;
- incorporated cities and towns;
- metropolitan governments;
- school districts;
- utility districts; and
- other political subdivisions of Tennessee.

By January 1, 2027, each covered local governmental entity must revise existing ordinances, resolutions, rules, policies, and procedures that reference a person's sex or gender so that they use the statutory definition.

Beginning July 1, 2026, a covered local entity may not adopt or enact a new ordinance, resolution, rule, policy, or procedure using a different definition.

### Local enforcement

Beginning January 1, 2027, a resident may file a complaint in the appropriate chancery court alleging that a local governmental entity is not complying.

The resident bears the burden of proving noncompliance by a preponderance of the evidence. When the court finds a violation, the statute directs the court to order compliance through mandamus.

If the local entity remains out of compliance ninety days after the court's order, the law makes it ineligible to enter grant contracts administered by the Tennessee Department of Economic and Community Development until compliance is certified.

The statute does not create a general monetary damages award for an individual complainant within this enforcement section.

### State governmental entities

The state provisions apply broadly to entities within Tennessee state government, including:

- executive-branch agencies;
- authorities;
- boards and commissions;
- departments and offices;
- autonomous state agencies; and
- public institutions of higher education.

By January 1, 2027, each covered state entity must revise existing rules, policies, and procedures that reference a natural person's sex or gender to use the statutory definition.

Beginning July 1, 2026, a covered state entity may not adopt a new rule, policy, or procedure using a different definition.

### State enforcement and funding consequences

Beginning January 1, 2027, a person may submit a complaint to the Tennessee Comptroller of the Treasury alleging that a state governmental entity is not complying.

The Comptroller may investigate a complaint the office considers credible. When an investigation finds noncompliance, the Comptroller provides notice and an opportunity for the entity to correct it.

If noncompliance continues for ninety days after notice:

- a covered entity that is eligible for Economic and Community Development grant contracts becomes ineligible for those contracts until compliance is certified; or
- when the entity is an institution of higher education or is otherwise outside that grant-eligibility mechanism, the Comptroller reports the matter to the Department of Finance and Administration and the General Assembly's Government Operations Committees.

For the latter group, the act establishes escalating notices and possible withholding of state general-fund revenue:

- the first notice warns of the consequence of continued noncompliance;
- after a second qualifying notice, ten percent of the entity's next-fiscal-year general-fund revenue may be withheld; and
- after a third qualifying notice, twenty percent may be withheld.

Withheld funds are held in escrow and may be released when the entity returns to compliance.

The statute does not explain every procedural detail for evaluating complaints, selecting records for review, challenging a Comptroller finding, or determining how system-level noncompliance will be measured.

### Identity-document carveout

Public Chapter 938 says a state or local governmental entity is not considered noncompliant **based solely on** accepting:

- a valid birth certificate; or
- another form of identification lawfully issued by a governmental entity.

This carveout is important. The statute does not, by itself:

- direct an agency to reject an otherwise valid identification document;
- order every Tennessee-issued identification card to be reissued;
- require every existing sex marker to be changed;
- establish a new digital-identification platform;
- specify whether an identity record may contain an additional field or internal annotation; or
- define how agencies must resolve conflicts between documents, databases, and current policy.

The phrase **based solely on** also means that acceptance of a valid document is protected from being the only basis for a noncompliance finding. The public record reviewed does not establish how courts or the Comptroller will analyze a broader policy or system that accepts such documents while also using other definitions or classifications.

### Federal-law limitation

The state-government section says the act does not require a state governmental entity to adopt or enact a rule, policy, or procedure that violates federal law.

The adopted amendment does not contain equally explicit federal-law language in the separate local-government section. This entry does not infer that local governments are permitted or required to violate controlling federal law. The interaction between the act and federal constitutional, statutory, regulatory, funding, or court requirements is a legal question that may depend on the entity and policy involved.

### What the law does not directly prescribe

Public Chapter 938 does not contain a technical specification for government identity systems.

It does not expressly prescribe:

- database column names or allowed field values;
- whether systems must use labels such as sex, gender, male, female, unknown, or another value;
- procedures for correcting an individual's record;
- treatment of historical records created under an earlier policy;
- migration, deletion, suppression, or archival requirements;
- rules for names, honorifics, pronouns, photographs, or signatures;
- identity-proofing standards;
- biometric collection or matching;
- document-scanning requirements;
- cross-agency record-matching logic;
- treatment of out-of-state or federal identification documents beyond the acceptance carveout;
- audit-log, access-control, retention, security, or breach-notification requirements; or
- a process for notifying an individual when a record or classification changes.

Those omissions do not mean systems will remain unchanged. They mean that technical changes must be established through later policies, implementation records, contracts, system documentation, or observed government practice rather than assumed from the statute alone.

## Government systems and implementation

Government definitions often become operational through information systems. A rule or policy may determine what appears in:

- online application forms;
- employee and student information systems;
- benefits and eligibility systems;
- licensing and credentialing systems;
- corrections and law-enforcement records;
- school enrollment and education records;
- public-health and administrative datasets;
- grant and contracting portals;
- identity-verification workflows;
- records-management systems;
- public dashboards and reports;
- data warehouses and analytics tools; and
- interfaces that exchange data among agencies or contractors.

Public Chapter 938 may therefore prompt data-governance work even though it does not create a named technology system. Possible implementation tasks could include reviewing policy language, form instructions, data definitions, field labels, validation rules, reports, interoperability mappings, and vendor configurations.

Those are reasonable technical implications of the mandate, not confirmed changes to every Tennessee system.

As of the last review date, this project did not locate a clearly labeled statewide implementation manual, data standard, model policy, database schema, form inventory, identity-document rule, system-change directive, or cross-agency technical bulletin specific to Public Chapter 938 from the Comptroller, Department of Finance and Administration, Strategic Technology Solutions, Department of Safety and Homeland Security, or the state's public higher-education systems.

## What officials say

The act includes legislative findings asserting that the state has an interest in maintaining consistent definitions of sex and gender in government and that differing definitions can cause confusion in laws, policies, records, and administration.

Senate sponsor Paul Rose framed the proposal as creating consistency and clarity by using what supporters describe as biological definitions of male and female across government.

Those statements describe the sponsors' policy rationale. They are not independent findings by this tracker about administrative efficiency, scientific consensus, civil-rights effects, technical cost, or the experiences of affected people.

The official fiscal analysis estimated that the act could affect state and local operations but did not establish a complete inventory or statewide cost for policy review, legal work, software changes, data migration, training, complaint handling, or funding enforcement.

## What advocates and critics say

Supporters describe Public Chapter 938 as a government-consistency measure. They argue that state and local policies should use a single birth-based definition of sex and gender and that government bodies should not adopt conflicting definitions.

LGBTQ advocates, including the Tennessee Equality Project, opposed the legislation. Critics argue that it may erase or exclude transgender, nonbinary, and intersex people from government policy; pressure agencies and local governments to remove inclusive language; complicate access to services and records; and create enforcement incentives tied to grants and state funding.

Critics also argue that the law's binary definition may not account for the full range of biological variation and that government systems frequently use sex or gender for different administrative, clinical, statistical, legal, or identity purposes.

These positions identify serious policy and implementation questions. They do not establish that every agency will make the same technical change or that every possible consequence has already occurred.

## What is confirmed?

- Governor Bill Lee signed HB 1271 on May 7, 2026.
- The bill became Public Chapter 938.
- The final House vote was 72-16 and the final Senate vote was 24-6.
- The adopted House amendment replaced the introduced bill's operative text.
- The act took effect when signed.
- Since July 1, 2026, covered state and local entities have been prohibited from adopting new covered materials using a conflicting definition.
- Existing covered ordinances, resolutions, rules, policies, and procedures must be revised by January 1, 2027.
- The statutory definition describes sex or gender as immutable reproductive-system characteristics identifying a person as male or female based on anatomy and genetics existing at birth.
- The statute does not require an individual genetic test.
- The local provisions cover counties, municipalities, metropolitan governments, school districts, utility districts, and other political subdivisions.
- The state provisions cover a broad range of agencies, boards, offices, authorities, autonomous entities, and public higher-education institutions.
- Local enforcement may begin through chancery-court complaints on January 1, 2027.
- State enforcement may begin through complaints to the Comptroller on January 1, 2027.
- Continued noncompliance can affect Economic and Community Development grant eligibility.
- Continued noncompliance by certain state entities or public higher-education institutions can lead to escalating notices and possible withholding of general-fund revenue.
- A state or local entity is not noncompliant based solely on accepting a valid birth certificate or other lawfully issued government identification.
- The law does not prescribe a specific database schema, identity platform, form design, individual record-correction process, historical-data rule, or technical security control.

Potentially affected groups and institutions include:

- transgender, nonbinary, intersex, and gender-nonconforming people interacting with Tennessee government;
- people whose identity documents, birth records, out-of-state documents, or federal records contain differing sex or gender information;
- residents applying for licenses, benefits, education, employment, housing, public services, or government programs;
- state employees and applicants;
- students, employees, and applicants at public colleges and universities;
- K-12 students, families, and school personnel;
- counties, cities, school districts, utility districts, and other political subdivisions;

## Privacy and civil-liberties significance

Public Chapter 938 is a government data-governance law as well as an LGBTQ and transgender policy law.

Definitions shape how government systems classify people. Once a definition is embedded in forms, databases, portals, reports, and interoperability rules, it can affect what information a person may submit, how records are matched, which values are accepted, what appears to government employees, and how eligibility or compliance decisions are made.

The statute's identity-document carveout may reduce one direct risk by preventing acceptance of a valid government-issued document from being the sole basis for a noncompliance finding. But the law does not explain what happens when a document, database, policy, and individual record do not align.

Implementation could create privacy risks if agencies begin collecting additional birth, medical, anatomical, or documentary evidence to classify records. It could also create accuracy and due-process risks if systems overwrite historical information, reject lawful documents, create duplicate identities, or change records without notice and a correction process.

The law does not require those outcomes. They are risks to monitor because the statute creates a broad mandate without a statewide technical standard, data-minimization rule, individual-notice requirement, appeal process, or records-correction procedure.

The most defensible current conclusion is:

> Public Chapter 938 clearly requires state and local government policies to use a birth-based binary definition of sex and gender and creates meaningful enforcement consequences. It does not itself establish a digital-identification system or order every individual record changed. The public record does not yet show how Tennessee entities will translate the mandate into forms, databases, identity workflows, historical records, data exchanges, or individual correction procedures.
