## Research and legal limitations

This entry documents a confirmed Tennessee-to-federal voter-data transfer and the public statements surrounding it. It does not have access to the transmitted file itself or to nonpublic DOJ or Tennessee systems.

The public record located for this review does not provide Tennessee's complete transmission manifest, exact transfer date, transfer mechanism, executed data-use agreement, retention schedule, contractor-access terms, or a Tennessee-specific log showing what happened to the file after DOJ received it. Those gaps are identified rather than filled by inference.

The entry also distinguishes DOJ's general, publicly described plan for using state voter files with the federal SAVE citizenship-verification system from a Tennessee-specific fact. No public record located in the Special Case research proves that Tennessee's particular DOJ copy was processed through that workflow.

## What happened?

In late 2025, the U.S. Department of Justice sought statewide voter-registration lists from states as part of a nationwide voter-roll initiative. On December 18, 2025, DOJ publicly said Tennessee had announced its intent to voluntarily provide its full voter-registration list.

By January 2026, Tennessee reporting described the transfer as completed. The strongest direct confirmation came on August 5, 2026, when Tennessee Secretary of State Tre Hargett said in a PBS NewsHour interview that state officials consulted counsel, were advised that DOJ had a right to the information, and then provided it.

Tennessee Elections Coordinator Mark Goins independently told Stateline that Tennessee had shared its voter data after state officials concluded DOJ was legally entitled to it.

Goins also said Tennessee **did not sign DOJ's proposed memorandum of understanding** governing voter-roll analysis and follow-up. He said state officials were concerned that proposed removal requirements could conflict with the National Voter Registration Act and create false-positive removal risks.

Public reporting consistently describes the Tennessee file as including voter names, dates of birth, addresses, and the last four digits of Social Security numbers. Driver's-license or DMV identifiers are also strongly supported by public reporting and national summaries, but the actual Tennessee transmission manifest has not been located. No evidence reviewed supports a claim that Tennessee transmitted full Social Security numbers.

## What the primary source says

The primary source for this entry is the August 5 PBS NewsHour interview with Secretary Hargett because it provides direct, on-record confirmation from Tennessee's chief election official that the state actually gave the requested voter information to DOJ.

Hargett's statement establishes the completed transfer at a level the earlier December 2025 DOJ announcement did not: DOJ's announcement established Tennessee's **intent** to provide the list, while Hargett later confirmed the information was in fact provided.

The interview also discusses sensitive identifiers, including the last four digits of Social Security numbers and driver's-license information, in the context of Tennessee voter data. Because the complete Tennessee file specification has not been published, this entry does not treat every described field as independently proven by a contemporaneous transmission record.

## What officials say

DOJ has said its voter-roll requests are part of enforcing federal election laws and evaluating whether states are maintaining accurate voter-registration lists. In its December 2025 announcement, DOJ described Tennessee as one of the states intending to cooperate voluntarily.

Hargett said Tennessee provided the information after relying on legal advice that DOJ was entitled to receive it. His public explanation did not characterize the transfer as an endorsement of every part of DOJ's broader voter-roll program.

Goins gave an important qualification: Tennessee declined DOJ's proposed MOU. According to his on-record explanation, Tennessee officials were concerned about voter-removal provisions and the possibility that false matches could lead to improper removals among a statewide registration list of roughly four million voters.

DOJ's Office of Legal Counsel later described DOJ's legal position and a general workflow in which collected state voter files may be retained in a Civil Rights Division system of records and used with a DOJ-controlled SAVE account involving DHS personnel. That is a federal description of DOJ's authority and intended workflow; it is not a Tennessee-specific processing log.

## What advocates and critics say

Voting-rights and privacy organizations have challenged DOJ's nationwide collection of complete voter files, particularly where the requested data include information such as birth dates and partial Social Security or driver's-license identifiers. Their concerns include statutory authority, concentration of sensitive voter data, secondary use, retention, and the risk of erroneous database matches affecting eligible voters.

Tennessee's own Elections Coordinator raised a narrower institutional concern from inside state government: Goins said Tennessee declined the proposed MOU because of concern about voter-removal requirements and false positives.

Those objections do not establish that DOJ has misused Tennessee's file, that an eligible Tennessee voter has been removed because of the transfer, or that the transfer itself altered any vote or ballot. They identify legal and data-governance questions that remain relevant while the federal voter-roll program continues.

## What is confirmed?

- DOJ publicly announced in December 2025 that Tennessee intended to voluntarily provide its full statewide voter-registration list.
- Secretary of State Tre Hargett later directly confirmed that Tennessee actually provided the requested voter information to DOJ.
- Elections Coordinator Mark Goins separately confirmed that Tennessee shared voter data with DOJ.
- Tennessee did not sign DOJ's proposed voter-roll MOU, according to Goins.
- Public evidence strongly supports that the transferred data included names, dates of birth, addresses, and the last four digits of Social Security numbers.
- Driver's-license or DMV identifiers are strongly supported as part of the requested or transferred data, but the exact Tennessee manifest remains unavailable.
- No evidence reviewed supports a claim that full Social Security numbers were transmitted.
- Tennessee's ordinary public voter-registration record excludes Social Security numbers, driver's-license numbers, and Department of Safety and Homeland Security ID numbers, meaning the sensitive identifiers at issue are not simply the ordinary public-facing voter file.

## What remains uncertain?

- The exact date and technical method of Tennessee's file transfer to DOJ.
- The complete field-by-field Tennessee transmission manifest.
- Whether voting history or any additional fields were included or excluded.
- The text and effect of a Tennessee-related DOJ privacy letter referenced in released federal correspondence.
- Whether a separate Tennessee-specific security, retention, deletion, breach-notification, contractor-access, or secondary-use agreement governed the transfer.
- How long DOJ will retain Tennessee's copy and which personnel or contractors can access it.
- Whether Tennessee's particular DOJ file has been processed through DOJ's SAVE workflow, accessed by DHS personnel, disclosed to HSI or another federal component, or used to produce a match-results file returned to Tennessee.
- Whether any later federal or Tennessee action based on this transferred copy has affected an individual voter's registration status.

## Who may be affected?

The most directly affected population is Tennessee's statewide registered-voter population because the transferred file concerns voter-registration records maintained by the state.

The privacy implications are greater for records containing nonpublic identifiers such as partial Social Security or driver's-license information than they would be for an ordinary public voter list. The present public record does not establish that individual voters must take any action because their information was transferred.

If DOJ or another federal component later uses Tennessee's file for eligibility or citizenship matching, people whose state and federal records differ could be affected by follow-up review. No Tennessee-specific downstream process from the DOJ copy is established in this entry, so that possibility remains conditional rather than a confirmed effect.

## Privacy and civil-liberties significance

This development belongs in the Tracker because it moves a statewide election dataset—including sensitive identifying fields strongly supported by the public record—from Tennessee election administration into a federal voter-roll program.

The central digital-rights questions are about **data governance**: what exactly was transferred, under what authority, which systems can access or match it, how long it is retained, whether it can be shared onward, how erroneous matches are corrected, and what notice or review occurs before a database result affects an eligible voter.

The absence of a signed DOJ MOU is also relevant because safeguards contained in agreements signed by other states cannot automatically be attributed to Tennessee. The missing Tennessee-specific privacy and transmission records therefore remain a meaningful documentation gap rather than evidence that no safeguards exist.

## Lawful actions and resources

- Review the Tennessee Secretary of State's voter-registration and election information for current registration status and official election guidance.
- Read DOJ's public voter-roll announcements and the cited Office of Legal Counsel opinion for the federal government's stated legal position.
- Contact the Tennessee Secretary of State / Division of Elections with questions about what voter fields were transferred, retention or correction procedures, and whether later federal match results have been returned to Tennessee.
- Request public records concerning Tennessee's DOJ correspondence, privacy letter, transmission instructions, agreements, or implementation records where state law permits.
- For an individual voter-registration problem or legal question, consult the relevant county election commission, a qualified attorney, or an appropriate voting-rights organization.
