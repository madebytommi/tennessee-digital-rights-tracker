# Tennessee Digital Rights Tracker Research Protocol

## Purpose

This document defines the standard research workflow for the Tennessee Digital Rights Tracker.

It governs how researchers and AI agents investigate, verify, document, and prepare issues for inclusion in the Tracker after an issue has been identified as potentially relevant.

The goal is not simply to collect links or summarize reporting. The goal is to produce a reproducible, evidence-backed factual record that clearly distinguishes:

* documented facts;
* claims made by officials, vendors, advocates, critics, or other parties;
* reasonable analysis;
* unresolved questions; and
* the Tracker's own conclusions.

This protocol applies to human researchers and to AI-assisted research performed with tools such as ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini, Codex, browser agents, research agents, or similar systems.

The public-facing methodology in `docs/methodology.md` explains the Tracker's evidence standards. This protocol defines the operational workflow used to carry them out.

---

# 1. Entry Point

This protocol begins after a possible Tracker issue has been identified.

A lead may originate from:

* news reporting;
* social media;
* advocacy organizations;
* government announcements;
* legislation;
* court cases;
* public records;
* procurement databases;
* Atlas of Surveillance;
* tips from readers;
* other research projects; or
* AI-assisted discovery.

Before conducting full research, determine whether the issue plausibly falls within the scope of the Tennessee Digital Rights Tracker.

Examples include developments involving:

* government surveillance;
* automated license plate readers;
* facial recognition;
* biometric technologies;
* location tracking;
* government data collection;
* data sharing;
* digital privacy;
* cybersecurity policy;
* government databases;
* algorithmic or AI decision systems;
* online speech or censorship;
* digital access;
* identity systems;
* health-data privacy;
* reproductive-data privacy;
* LGBTQ or transgender digital-rights issues;
* education technology with privacy implications;
* immigration enforcement technologies;
* law-enforcement technology;
* digital due process;
* platform or communications surveillance;
* emerging technologies affecting civil liberties.

If the issue does not materially involve digital rights, civil liberties, surveillance, privacy, data governance, technology, or closely related rights, it should normally remain outside the Tracker.

---

# 2. Define the Research Question

Before gathering evidence, convert the lead into a concrete research question.

Do not begin with the assumption that the original claim is true.

Good research questions ask what can actually be established.

Examples:

> Has Springfield, Tennessee purchased, deployed, approved, or considered Flock Safety automated license plate readers?

> What data-sharing authority does this Tennessee law give to state agencies?

> Is this surveillance program currently operational, merely proposed, or no longer active?

> What does this court ruling actually change for Tennessee residents?

The research question should identify, where relevant:

* jurisdiction;
* agency or government body;
* technology, law, policy, contract, or program;
* relevant dates;
* current operational or legal status;
* responsible decision-makers;
* affected populations;
* privacy or civil-liberties implications;
* implementation details; and
* major unresolved questions.

---

# 3. Record the Original Lead

Preserve the discovery source before deeper research begins.

Record when possible:

* URL;
* title;
* publisher or source;
* publication date;
* retrieval date;
* author;
* relevant quoted or paraphrased claim;
* screenshots or archived copy where appropriate;
* why the lead appears relevant.

The original lead is a research starting point, not automatically evidence sufficient for publication.

News articles, social-media posts, advocacy materials, vendor pages, and third-party databases should normally be treated as discovery sources until their important claims are independently verified.

---

# 4. Build a Claim List

Identify the factual claims that would need to be true for the issue to be accurately represented in the Tracker.

Break large claims into smaller verifiable propositions.

For example:

Instead of researching:

> "Springfield uses Flock."

Break the question into:

* Did the city approve the system?
* Which agency operates it?
* Was a contract signed?
* Was money appropriated?
* Were cameras actually installed?
* How many cameras exist?
* Who owns the equipment?
* What vendor provides the system?
* What data is collected?
* How long is the data retained?
* Who can access it?
* Is data shared with other agencies?
* Is participation in a national or regional network enabled?
* Is there a written policy?
* Is the system currently active?
* What oversight exists?

Each material claim should eventually be classified as:

* verified;
* partially verified;
* contradicted;
* unresolved; or
* unsupported.

---

# 5. Follow the Evidence Hierarchy

Use the evidence hierarchy defined in `docs/methodology.md`.

Prefer sources in approximately this order:

1. enacted law, bill text, amendment, executive order, regulation, court opinion, contract, procurement record, meeting agenda, meeting packet, official minutes, official dataset, or equivalent primary record;
2. official agency, legislative, judicial, or local-government documentation;
3. direct statements from named officials, agencies, contractors, vendors, or organizations;
4. established civil-rights, civil-liberties, academic, legal, or technical analysis;
5. reputable news reporting;
6. social-media posts, screenshots, unsourced summaries, or aggregators.

Lower-tier sources may be useful for discovery but should not replace accessible higher-tier evidence.

---

# 6. Search Primary Sources First

Once the relevant government body, agency, law, program, or technology is known, search its official records directly.

Do not rely solely on ordinary web-search results.

Where relevant, examine:

* government websites;
* legislative databases;
* city and county websites;
* police or sheriff pages;
* board or commission pages;
* meeting agendas;
* meeting minutes;
* meeting packets;
* procurement records;
* purchasing portals;
* contracts;
* requests for proposals;
* bid awards;
* budget documents;
* ordinances;
* resolutions;
* policies;
* public notices;
* annual reports;
* court dockets;
* court opinions;
* executive orders;
* administrative rules;
* official press releases;
* agency guidance;
* datasets;
* public-record repositories.

Use the site's own search system where available.

When researching technologies or vendors, search multiple relevant terms and variants.

For example:

* Flock
* Flock Safety
* ALPR
* LPR
* automated license plate reader
* automatic license plate reader
* license plate recognition
* license plate camera
* surveillance camera
* camera system

Do not assume that government records use the terminology found in a news article.

---

# 7. Expand the Search Systematically

Research should move outward from the primary jurisdiction or agency.

A typical search order is:

1. named government entity;
2. responsible department or agency;
3. governing board or legislative body;
4. procurement and financial records;
5. relevant state agencies;
6. vendor documentation;
7. court or legislative records;
8. civil-liberties or technical analysis;
9. reputable journalism;
10. broader web discovery.

Search combinations involving:

* agency name;
* vendor;
* technology;
* contract;
* purchase;
* procurement;
* policy;
* retention;
* sharing;
* access;
* database;
* memorandum of understanding;
* grant;
* budget;
* council;
* commission;
* agenda;
* minutes;
* resolution;
* ordinance.

Search date ranges when the timing of adoption or implementation is known.

---

# 8. Verify Current Status Separately

Historical evidence does not automatically establish present status.

For any active Tracker entry, attempt to determine whether the issue is currently:

* proposed;
* approved;
* contracted;
* funded;
* deployed;
* operational;
* suspended;
* blocked;
* enjoined;
* repealed;
* expired;
* replaced; or
* unknown.

Do not infer current operation merely because a system was purchased or approved.

Likewise, do not infer that a program ended merely because recent documentation cannot be located.

If present status cannot be established, say so.

---

# 9. Investigate Implementation Details

For technology-related issues, attempt to establish relevant implementation details.

Depending on the issue, investigate:

* vendor;
* product;
* number of devices;
* locations;
* cost;
* funding source;
* contract term;
* renewal provisions;
* data collected;
* retention period;
* access controls;
* authorized users;
* auditing;
* search capabilities;
* alerts;
* watchlists;
* data-sharing arrangements;
* regional or national network participation;
* third-party access;
* integration with other databases;
* public policy governing use;
* oversight;
* reporting requirements;
* complaint procedures;
* deletion procedures.

Do not assume that every feature advertised by a vendor is enabled by the Tennessee agency using that product.

A vendor's technical capabilities and a local agency's actual configuration must be treated as separate questions.

---

# 10. Investigate Legal and Policy Authority

Where relevant, identify the legal or policy authority under which the program operates.

Search for:

* statutes;
* ordinances;
* regulations;
* resolutions;
* agency policies;
* executive orders;
* contracts;
* memoranda of understanding;
* judicial decisions;
* attorney-general opinions;
* administrative guidance.

Separate:

* what the law permits;
* what the agency claims the law permits;
* what the agency actually does;
* what critics argue;
* what courts have ruled.

Do not present unresolved legal interpretation as settled law.

---

# 11. Triangulate Material Claims

Important factual claims should, whenever practical, be supported by more than one form of evidence.

Strong combinations include:

* contract + meeting minutes;
* statute + agency guidance;
* procurement record + official announcement;
* court opinion + docket;
* government record + reputable reporting;
* agency policy + vendor documentation.

Independent confirmation is especially important for claims involving:

* surveillance scope;
* data sharing;
* retention;
* government access;
* affected populations;
* enforcement;
* financial cost;
* operational status;
* legal effect.

Triangulation does not mean collecting multiple articles that all repeat the same underlying source.

Identify the original evidence whenever possible.

---

# 12. Track Source Provenance

For each material source, retain enough information for another researcher to locate and evaluate it.

Where feasible, record:

* source title;
* issuing organization;
* document date;
* publication date;
* retrieval date;
* original URL;
* archived URL;
* document filename;
* relevant page or section;
* relevant quotation or factual proposition;
* local archival filename;
* cryptographic hash.

If the source is a PDF, meeting packet, contract, or similar document, preserve page references for important claims.

---

# 13. Archive Evidence

Important evidence should be preserved where lawful and technically feasible.

Possible preservation methods include:

* Internet Archive;
* ArchiveWeb.page;
* local PDF;
* local HTML capture;
* screenshot;
* downloaded meeting packet;
* saved court document;
* hashed source file.

Preserve especially important or fragile sources such as:

* contracts;
* meeting packets;
* procurement pages;
* policies;
* government PDFs;
* pages likely to change;
* vendor announcements;
* local-government pages.

Do not republish sensitive personal information merely because it appears in a public document.

---

# 14. Distinguish Evidence From Claims

Every research conclusion should distinguish among the following categories.

## Documented fact

Directly supported by evidence.

Example:

> The city council approved a three-year contract on May 12, 2026.

## Official claim

A statement made by a government official, agency, contractor, or vendor.

Example:

> Police officials say the system will only be used for criminal investigations.

This is evidence that the officials made the statement, not necessarily proof that the statement accurately describes every use.

## External analysis

Interpretation from journalists, civil-rights organizations, researchers, attorneys, or technical experts.

## Tracker analysis

A reasoned conclusion drawn from the available evidence.

## Unknown

A question the available public record cannot answer.

Never collapse these categories together.

---

# 15. Record Contradictory Evidence

Do not discard evidence because it conflicts with the original lead or emerging narrative.

If sources disagree:

* identify the disagreement;
* determine whether the sources refer to different dates or versions;
* prefer primary evidence where appropriate;
* investigate whether circumstances changed;
* preserve the contradiction;
* lower confidence if it cannot be resolved.

The Tracker should follow the evidence even when the evidence weakens or overturns the premise that initiated the research.

---

# 16. Record Negative Findings Carefully

Failure to locate evidence is not proof that something does not exist.

Use language such as:

> No public policy governing retention was located during this review.

Do not convert this into:

> The department has no retention policy.

Likewise:

> No evidence was located showing the system is currently operational.

is different from:

> The system is not operational.

Absence of public evidence should be reported as an evidence limitation.

---

# 17. Assess Digital-Rights Significance

Once the facts are reasonably established, explain why the issue matters.

Potential implications may include:

* privacy;
* anonymity;
* freedom of expression;
* freedom of association;
* due process;
* equal protection;
* discrimination;
* chilling effects;
* surveillance;
* data security;
* identity exposure;
* government transparency;
* accountability;
* algorithmic decision-making;
* reproductive privacy;
* health-data privacy;
* immigration enforcement;
* LGBTQ or transgender safety;
* youth privacy;
* location privacy.

Distinguish demonstrated effects from plausible risks.

Do not state that a technology causes a specific harm unless evidence supports that conclusion.

---

# 18. Assign Confidence

Use the Tracker confidence levels.

## High

Use when:

* primary records directly establish the core facts;
* current status is clear;
* material claims are independently supported;
* little important ambiguity remains.

## Medium

Use when:

* the core development is well supported;
* some implementation details remain uncertain;
* current configuration or practical effect cannot be fully established.

## Low

Use when:

* evidence is preliminary;
* sources conflict;
* important records are unavailable;
* the claim rests mostly on secondary reporting;
* current status cannot be verified.

Low-confidence material must not be framed as settled fact.

---

# 19. Assign Status

Use the status categories defined by the Tracker.

## Proposed

Introduced or under consideration but not currently in force or operation.

## Monitoring

Implemented or documented, but important current facts remain unresolved or the situation is actively developing.

## Active

Currently in effect or operation based on reasonably current evidence.

## Blocked or Enjoined

Prevented from taking effect or continuing by a court or other competent authority.

## Repealed, Expired, or Resolved

No longer active but retained for historical or accountability purposes.

Status should reflect evidence, not assumptions.

---

# 20. Determine Whether Research Is Publication-Ready

Research is normally ready for a Tracker entry when the following questions can be answered with reasonable confidence:

* What happened?
* Who did it?
* Where?
* When?
* What is the current status?
* What primary evidence supports it?
* What does it mean in plain language?
* Why does it matter for digital rights?
* What important facts remain unknown?
* What confidence level is appropriate?

Not every implementation detail must be known.

Unknowns may remain if they are explicitly documented.

Do not indefinitely delay publication because perfect information is unavailable.

---

# 21. Prepare the Tracker Entry

Only after the underlying research is reasonably stable should the public-facing Tracker entry be drafted.

The entry should be derived from the evidence record rather than written first and researched afterward.

All public-facing Tracker prose must also follow `docs/editorial-policy.md`, including its rules for tone, motive attribution, predictions, headlines, and corrections.

The entry should include, as appropriate:

* concise title;
* jurisdiction;
* category;
* status;
* relevant dates;
* plain-language summary;
* documented facts;
* digital-rights significance;
* affected groups;
* key sources;
* confidence;
* unresolved questions;
* update history.

Avoid unnecessary rhetoric.

Strong evidence is more persuasive than dramatic language.

---

# 22. Citation Standards

Every material factual statement should be traceable to a source.

Prefer direct citations to:

* legislation;
* official records;
* contracts;
* court documents;
* policies;
* government pages.

Use secondary reporting where it provides:

* context;
* interviews;
* chronology;
* information unavailable in public records.

Do not cite an aggregator when the original source is available.

Do not cite search-result snippets as evidence.

---

# 23. Final Verification Pass

Before publication, conduct a final verification review.

Confirm:

* names;
* agencies;
* jurisdictions;
* dates;
* dollar amounts;
* bill numbers;
* court names;
* case numbers;
* contract terms;
* technology names;
* device counts;
* quotations;
* URLs;
* status;
* confidence.

Check that important claims are supported by the cited source rather than merely related to it.

Look specifically for accidental overstatement.

Especially strong legal or technical conclusions should be independently reviewed by an appropriately qualified expert before publication when practical.

---

# 24. Adversarial Review

Before publication, ask:

> What evidence would prove this entry wrong?

> What claim in this entry has the weakest support?

> Are we presenting an allegation as a fact?

> Are we inferring current status from old evidence?

> Are we assuming a vendor feature is locally enabled?

> Are we implying that failure to locate a policy means no policy exists?

> Are multiple sources actually repeating the same original source?

> Is there another reasonable interpretation of the evidence?

> Have we accurately represented the strongest credible opposing explanation?

Correct weaknesses before publishing.

---

# 25. Publication and Repository Validation

Before merging or publishing:

* validate required front matter or structured fields;
* verify allowed taxonomy values;
* confirm links;
* check formatting;
* run repository validation or tests;
* confirm the site builds successfully;
* review the rendered entry when practical.

Research quality and technical validity are separate requirements.

An entry should satisfy both.

---

# 26. Post-Publication Monitoring

Publication does not end the research lifecycle.

Review entries when:

* legislation changes;
* a court rules;
* an agency changes policy;
* a contract is renewed or terminated;
* new procurement records appear;
* new public records become available;
* implementation begins;
* deployment expands;
* a factual correction is received;
* reliable reporting contradicts the entry;
* a relevant Atlas of Surveillance record changes;
* six months pass without review on an active issue.

Material changes should be reflected in the entry's revision history where supported by the Tracker structure.

---

# 27. AI Research Rules

AI tools may assist with:

* discovery;
* search-term generation;
* document analysis;
* source comparison;
* extraction;
* timeline construction;
* identifying contradictions;
* drafting research notes;
* identifying missing evidence;
* preparing Tracker entries.

AI tools must not be treated as evidence.

An AI-generated statement is not a source.

AI researchers must:

* follow this protocol;
* distinguish discovery from verification;
* open and inspect underlying sources;
* prefer primary records;
* preserve source provenance;
* identify uncertainties;
* avoid inventing citations;
* avoid filling missing facts through inference;
* report contradictory evidence;
* state when a source could not be accessed;
* never claim to have inspected a document that was not actually opened.

Search-engine summaries and AI-generated search answers are discovery aids only.

---

# 28. Browser-Agent Rules

Browser agents should actively navigate relevant official websites rather than relying solely on search-engine results.

When researching a city, county, police department, board, commission, or agency, browser agents should inspect relevant sections such as:

* agendas;
* minutes;
* packets;
* public safety;
* procurement;
* contracts;
* policies;
* budgets;
* ordinances;
* resolutions;
* public notices.

Use internal website search functions when available.

Search multiple synonyms and vendor/product terms.

Record important URLs and document names.

Browser agents should report both what they found and what they searched without finding.

---

# 29. Research-Agent Handoff

When one tool performs discovery and another performs verification, preserve the chain of evidence.

A useful handoff should include:

* issue being investigated;
* research question;
* current hypothesis;
* claims needing verification;
* sources already located;
* primary records located;
* unresolved questions;
* contradictory evidence;
* recommended next searches.

Do not force each agent to rediscover the project from scratch.

---

# 30. Stop Conditions

Research may stop when:

* the core Tracker-relevant facts are verified;
* important primary sources have been examined;
* major contradictions have been resolved or documented;
* remaining unknowns are explicitly identified;
* additional searching is producing diminishing returns;
* the confidence level accurately reflects remaining uncertainty.

Research should not continue indefinitely merely to increase source count.

Quality matters more than volume.

---

# 31. Research Failure Conditions

An issue should normally not be published as a factual Tracker entry when:

* the central claim cannot be verified;
* available evidence materially contradicts the proposed entry;
* the issue rests almost entirely on anonymous or unverifiable claims;
* the Tennessee connection cannot be established;
* the digital-rights relevance is too weak;
* the available evidence is so incomplete that publication would be misleading.

In such cases, retain the issue as a research lead if appropriate.

A failed verification is a legitimate research outcome.

---

# 32. Core Research Principle

The Tracker does not research in order to prove its starting assumption.

The Tracker researches in order to determine what the evidence supports.

The final conclusion may:

* confirm the original lead;
* narrow it;
* qualify it;
* substantially change it;
* contradict it; or
* remain unresolved.

The evidence determines the entry.

The entry does not determine the evidence.

---

# 33. Standard Workflow Summary

The default research lifecycle is:

**Discovery**

→ **Scope and eligibility decision**

→ **Define research question**

→ **Preserve original lead**

→ **Build claim list**

→ **Search primary records**

→ **Expand supporting research**

→ **Verify current status**

→ **Investigate implementation and legal authority**

→ **Triangulate material claims**

→ **Archive evidence and provenance**

→ **Document contradictions and unknowns**

→ **Assess digital-rights significance**

→ **Assign confidence and status**

→ **Adversarial review**

→ **Draft Tracker entry**

→ **Final factual verification**

→ **Repository validation**

→ **Publish**

→ **Monitor for updates**

---

# 34. Governing Principle

When this protocol conflicts with convenience, speed, search-engine summaries, AI-generated answers, or the desire to publish quickly, this protocol takes priority.

A smaller number of well-supported Tracker entries is preferable to a larger number of poorly verified entries.
