# Taxonomy

Each published entry should have one primary category from the validated category list below.

## `lgbtq-trans-policy`

Government actions affecting legal recognition, healthcare, education, employment, public accommodations, identity documents, speech, or participation in public life.

## `government-surveillance`

License-plate readers, facial recognition, camera systems, device searches, geofence practices, social-media monitoring, public-private surveillance partnerships, and data-sharing systems.

## `health-data-privacy`

Collection, reporting, sale, sharing, subpoena, retention, or secondary use of medical and health-adjacent data.

## `online-identity-age-verification`

Age gates, identity verification, digital-ID mandates, biometric checks, platform obligations, and access restrictions.

## `government-ai-automation`

AI-assisted investigations, eligibility systems, risk scoring, fraud detection, content monitoring, automated decision systems, and algorithmic procurement.

## `digital-civic-information`

Digital systems, networks, technologies, and data practices that materially shape how Tennesseans encounter, evaluate, or receive information about elections, government, public policy, or civic participation.

The central concern must involve the technical or infrastructural means by which civic information is created, transformed, targeted, authenticated, distributed, amplified, or obscured.

### Core scope test

Ask:

> Would this still be a digital-rights or civic-infrastructure issue if the political viewpoint, party, candidate, or ideology were reversed?

If yes, the issue may fit this category. If the concern disappears once the political viewpoint is removed, it is probably ordinary political or media criticism and should remain outside the Tracker.

### In scope

An issue may fit when it has a meaningful Tennessee nexus and involves one or more of the following:

- automated or AI-assisted creation, rewriting, summarization, republication, or distribution of civic or political information;
- synthetic or materially manipulated political audio, video, images, text, or personas;
- unclear information provenance, including difficulty determining who created, funded, commissioned, transformed, or distributed apparently independent civic content;
- opaque sponsorship of news-like political content by campaigns, PACs, nonprofits, advocacy organizations, vendors, or other entities;
- coordinated or systematic digital distribution across multiple sites, accounts, platforms, or geographic audiences;
- use of personal, voter, consumer, location, demographic, health-adjacent, or other data to target Tennessee residents with civic or electoral messages;
- Tennessee state or local government use, purchase, regulation, monitoring, or contracting involving technology that affects the civic information environment;
- digital political-advertising, recommendation, disclosure, distribution, authentication, or provenance systems; or
- Tennessee legislation, regulation, litigation, election guidance, or enforcement concerning synthetic political media, political-content disclosure, digital advertising, provenance, or related technologies.

### Out of scope by itself

The following do not qualify without an additional technology, data, automation, provenance, or infrastructure issue:

- a politician making a false, misleading, inflammatory, or controversial statement;
- ordinary partisan journalism or candidate endorsements;
- editorial bias or political framing;
- individual social-media posts;
- ordinary campaign advertising with clear sponsorship;
- criticism of a news outlet solely because of its political perspective;
- general misinformation or disinformation; or
- political extremism considered solely as ideology.

The Tracker documents systems, technologies, data practices, disclosure mechanisms, and public policies. It does not determine which political viewpoints are acceptable.

### Tennessee nexus

A national platform, media system, or technology does not qualify merely because Tennesseans can access it. Research should establish at least one meaningful Tennessee connection, such as:

- a Tennessee candidate, campaign, PAC, election, ballot measure, or government body is directly involved;
- Tennessee residents are specifically targeted;
- a Tennessee-specific site, network, dataset, or distribution channel is involved;
- a Tennessee government entity purchases, operates, regulates, or investigates the system;
- Tennessee law governs the practice; or
- documented activity is specifically directed toward a Tennessee civic or electoral audience.

### Evidence and motive boundaries

Researchers must distinguish separately between:

- existence of the system;
- source of the underlying content;
- method of production or distribution;
- sponsorship or funding;
- coordination;
- intended audience;
- political effect; and
- motive.

Evidence that a system amplified political content does not by itself establish payment, coordination, ideological intent, suppression, manipulation, or electoral effect.

### Relationship to other categories

Use another primary category when it more directly describes the issue. For example, government facial-recognition monitoring of protesters belongs under `government-surveillance`, while AI determining government benefits eligibility belongs under `government-ai-automation`.

Use `digital-civic-information` when the central research issue is the creation, provenance, targeting, authentication, or distribution of civic information itself.

The secondary tag `civic-information` may still be used when civic-information concerns are important to an entry whose primary category belongs elsewhere, such as a political-deepfake entry primarily categorized under `government-ai-automation`.

## `election-systems-data`

Government technology, databases, identity-verification systems, cybersecurity systems, ballot-transmission infrastructure, voting-system certification, voter-record sharing, automated eligibility processes, and related technical or data systems used to administer elections.

Use this category when the core digital-rights question concerns how an election is technically administered rather than how political information is created or distributed.

### In scope

An issue may fit when it has a meaningful Tennessee nexus and involves one or more of the following:

- voter-registration databases or voter-file sharing;
- citizenship or eligibility-verification systems used for election administration;
- automated or database-assisted voter-list maintenance;
- election cybersecurity systems or federal-state election-security infrastructure;
- electronic poll books, ballot-tracking systems, or ballot-transmission infrastructure;
- voting-system testing, certification, standards, or technical oversight;
- technical audits, access controls, logging, or data-sharing rules for election systems;
- government use of federal identity, immigration, or benefits databases for voter eligibility decisions; or
- Tennessee implementation of federal election-technology, voter-data, or ballot-processing requirements.

### Out of scope by itself

The following do not qualify without a material technology, data, identity-system, cybersecurity, or digital-infrastructure component:

- ordinary campaign strategy;
- candidate or party disputes;
- political rhetoric about election integrity;
- general allegations of voter fraud;
- election-result disputes considered solely as political or legal controversies;
- polling, endorsements, fundraising, or get-out-the-vote activity; or
- ordinary election-law changes that do not materially involve data or technical infrastructure.

The category must not be used to imply that a technical change is improper, partisan, fraudulent, or outcome-determinative without evidence supporting that conclusion.

### Relationship to `digital-civic-information`

Use `election-systems-data` when the central issue is the technical administration of voting, voter eligibility, voter records, ballot handling, or election-system security.

Use `digital-civic-information` when the central issue is political-media provenance, targeting, authentication, synthetic content, or distribution of civic information.

An entry may use secondary tags to reflect overlap, but it should have only one primary category.
